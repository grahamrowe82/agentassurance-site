The Chainsaw

The chainsaw

When you work with AI it feels like holding a magic wand. You are holding a chainsaw. The reason everyone gets sucked in is that it all seems so innocent and so generous. I can make a piece of software that does this, and now it does that, and now it does this too. For a while you are frolicking in a meadow of endless creativity, and nothing about the experience warns you.

What gets your attention

All work has the same shape. The things that work get ignored and the things that break get all of your attention. There is no way round this, because the things that are smooth and working need nothing from you, and the things that are broken and dangerous demand you. It is how human teams go, how machines and industrial processes go, and how flows of information go.

So the first stretch of work with AI is always pleasant and inventive, and then it becomes extremely difficult, and it does not matter how good the models get. You always reach the point where the system starts to drown in its own waste, and the mistakes and the problems take up more and more of your day. Building things is great fun, and maintaining them is hell.

Why you cannot walk it back

The real trouble is that the things you built are so useful. By the time you have a working process that would have cost a fortune from professional developers a few years ago, or a database holding every customer you have, it is very hard to give it up. And by then you are running risks around security, compliance, correctness, and money that you do not understand and were never equipped to handle, at an intellectual level, a psychological level, or a professional one.

Every step that got you there was innocent, ethical, and correct, which is why none of it tripped any of the AI's own filters. Somehow you have still ended up with important and sensitive information flowing through channels you do not begin to understand. What started as an afternoon making a powerful piece of software can turn, in a blink, into months of security questionnaires and documents about how to construct documents, and minutiae you never imagined you would have to know. And once you have taken the pill, there is no going back.

It has happened before

There is some comfort in the fact that this has all happened before, and the most recent time was spreadsheets. In the 1990s anyone with a desk could build one, and business ran on them within a decade, most of them built by the person with the problem and never seen by anyone else. Then the failures started to surface.

In 2012 the model that JPMorgan's chief investment office used to measure the risk of its trading book ran in a set of Excel spreadsheets, filled in by hand by copying and pasting from one to the next. A cell divided by a sum where it should have divided by an average, which halved the reported volatility. The office lost more than six billion dollars that year, and the bank's own report on the affair described the spreadsheet in those terms.

In October 2020, in the middle of the pandemic, Public Health England lost 15,841 positive test results over eight days because the lab data was being loaded into an old Excel format that stops at 65,536 rows. The people who should have been told to isolate were not told, and the Health Secretary explained the row limit to the House of Commons.

The answer, when it came, was audit. After the accounting scandals of the early 2000s, firms were made to find every spreadsheet their financial reporting depended on, write down who owned it, and control changes to it. It felt like bureaucracy imposed on a tool everyone loved. It was also the first time most of those firms knew what their numbers were actually running on.

The same medicine is coming for everyone who has been pressing enter, and from where they stand it looks like the worst part of the whole business.
