How Other Industries Cleaned Up

Steam

Here is where the forms came from, told properly. The first machine in America that could kill people by the hundred was the steam boiler. Before 1852 boiler explosions caused about half of the seven thousand steamboat deaths in the United States. In 1838, the worst year, 342 people died in twelve explosions. Through the 1850s a boiler blew up somewhere in the country roughly once every four days.

The worst of them came at the end of the Civil War. At three in the morning on 27 April 1865, seven miles north of Memphis, three of the four boilers on the steamboat Sultana exploded. More than 1,500 people died, most of them Union soldiers on their way home from prison camps, and it is still the worst maritime disaster in American history. Nobody ever found out why, and there was nobody whose job it was to find out.

Government moved first, with laws in 1838 and 1852 that between them created the first federal agency ever to regulate a private industry. Engineers were already learning, and safety per mile was improving before the law. What the law added was the part that no longer depended on any one engineer choosing to be careful.

The commercial side started as a discussion group. In 1857 a few Hartford businessmen formed a club to talk about practical changes to boilers. Nine years later that circle became the Hartford Steam Boiler Inspection and Insurance Company. The idea is in the name: the company inspected your boiler and insured it, so the standard of inspection was set by the people who paid when the inspection was wrong.

The written standard came last, and engineers wrote it. The American Society of Mechanical Engineers was founded in 1880, a year in which 159 boilers exploded. The trigger for a code came a quarter of a century later, when boilers blew up in two Massachusetts shoe factories, at Brockton in 1905 and Lynn in 1906, and the governor used his inaugural address to demand action. In 1911 the society's president, who ran a boiler company himself, proposed that the society write a code.

The council minutes of 15 September 1911 record the committee, seven men, by name, appointed to write a standard for the construction of boilers and pressure vessels. Their chairman had sat on the Massachusetts board that regulated boilers. The first Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code came out in 1914. Put the dates in a row: thousands dead before 1852, the laws in 1838 and 1852, the inspection company in 1866, the engineers' society in 1880, the code in 1914. A century of explosions went into one document, and every stage of it was written in the language of particular disasters.

Rail

Rail ran the same sequence and gave us two more things: the organisation chart and the accident investigation. In 1841 two trains collided on the Western Railroad in Massachusetts, killing a conductor and a passenger. The railroad's answer was a document called the Report on Avoiding Collisions and Governing Employees, which set out who was responsible for what and who reported to whom. The thing you meet in every org chart and every audit interview was invented to stop trains hitting each other.

Britain built the accident inspectorate first. From 1840 the Board of Trade had an inspector for new railways, and an Act of 1871 gave it the power to investigate accidents and say what caused them, which the Sultana never got. Every air crash investigation since descends from it.

In America the new Interstate Commerce Commission published its first accident statistics in 1889, and the tables showed how many brakemen were being killed coupling cars by hand. In 1893 Congress mandated automatic couplers and air brakes, and the law followed the count.

Rail also produced the audit. The boom of the 1840s built companies larger and faster than any owner could see whole, and what they said about themselves drifted from what was true. An Act of 1844 required directors to draw up a full and fair balance sheet and shareholders to appoint auditors to check it, the first statutory audit anywhere. A few years later George Hudson, the Railway King, was found to have paid dividends out of capital with the accounts doctored to make it look earned. The accountants formed a profession around the requirement within a generation.

Electricity

Electricity was cleaned up faster, because the insurers had learned the lesson and applied it at the start. The 1893 World's Fair in Chicago was wired with untested electrical installations, and a young inspector named William Henry Merrill was sent to check them. With backing from two fire insurance associations and 350 dollars of equipment, he set up a testing laboratory and ran his first test in March 1894. That laboratory became Underwriters Laboratories. The insurers paid for it because electrical fires were their losses, and a tested mark was worth more to them than the testing cost.

The oldest example of all is older than steam. Lloyd's Register began in 1760 as a society that graded merchant ships so that the people insuring and chartering them could judge what they were dealing with, the best rated A1. Shipowners paid for the survey because without it nobody would insure the ship or charter it. Every layer since has had the same three parts: the people who know, the people who pay, and a counterparty who will not deal without the certificate.

Where the risk never closes

Steam, rail, and electricity have a happy ending: the danger was engineered out. A boiler built to the code does not explode, and a tested appliance does not start the fire. The risk in AI has no such ending. An action that reaches the world cannot be recalled, and the people who build the models say the exposure to hostile instructions cannot be fully closed. So the more useful precedents are the industries that have spent a century managing a risk that never goes away.

Audit is the closest, and the word assurance comes from it. An auditor does not certify that the accounts are right. The standard promises reasonable assurance, defined as high but not absolute, because some misstatement can escape even a properly conducted audit. A whole profession has been accountable and insured for over a century on that stated limit.

Aviation closed what engineering could close and then managed the rest, which was the crew. In December 1978 a United Airlines DC-8 ran out of fuel circling Portland while the captain worked on a landing-gear problem and did not act on the warnings of two junior officers who had watched the gauges fall. Ten people died in the crash. The investigation produced the discipline now called crew resource management, and the first airline programme ran in 1981.

Medicine has never claimed to close its risk at all. A Boston surgeon named Ernest Codman started tracking his own outcomes, deaths included, at the start of the twentieth century, and the morbidity and mortality conference that grew from it is a permanent review of what went wrong, still held every week in hospitals everywhere.

Cybersecurity is the youngest and the most like us. It has a workforce of around five and a half million people, by the 2024 ISC2 count, and after decades it has barely started to become a profession: the UK's chartered council, set up in 2022, registered its first hundred or so practitioners, and a US National Academies review said licensing was premature because the field was too broad and moved too fast. It became a large, lasting market of competing standards and certifications instead, and I expect the same of ours.

Where we are

Our incident record has started and it is thin: fabricated cases reaching courts, a fund charging fees for three years on a broken model, regulators finding improvised systems in supervised firms. These are our 1841 collision, and the systems that would fail at the Sultana's scale, fleets of agents inside critical processes, are being built now.

The standards came before the incidents this time, which is a departure. NIST published its AI risk framework in January 2023, describing it as voluntary. ISO 42001 followed the same year. They are written in the language of principle, in advance. The boiler code was written in the language of two shoe factories, afterwards, and nobody yet knows whether the other order works.

The commercial side has begun. In February 2026 the first certification standard for AI agents got its first authorised auditor, and in the same month a voice-agent company became the first firm insured against the certificate. Lloyd's syndicates are writing agent cover with limits of twenty-five million dollars and more. But the standard, the auditor, and the insurers are three separate businesses, and nobody yet inspects agent work and pays when the inspection is wrong, the way the Hartford did.

On the practitioners' side there is no code at all, and what the public record shows is people talking. That is 1857, a few people in a club in Hartford, discussing boilers.
