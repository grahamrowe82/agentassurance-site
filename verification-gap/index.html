Chapter 2: Trust

Trust

AI has made something visible that was always true. Everything we do all day runs on trust in other people. It is so ordinary a part of life that we do not notice it, and it has taken a machine that writes fluent English to make it show.

Take the most mundane object in the house, a kettle or a teabag. Understanding how either one works, and the expertise that went into procuring, processing, transporting, storing, and producing it at a price you would pay, and getting it into your hands safe and reliable, would take years of serious study. Nobody does that study before boiling the kettle. Everything we use, consume, and take part in during a day is like this: we could not possibly understand it, and we do not need to, because we trust it.

That trust is a real economic thing. It accumulates in brands and in companies through repeated dealings: we learn that certain things work and certain others fail, and the learning is worth money, because without it nothing can be done safely or reliably. If people lost the ability to trust things they cannot understand, the whole of society and the economy would fall apart. The point is easy to forget because it is the water we swim in, and it is fundamental to human life.

Habits of trust

We have different habits of trust in different parts of life. When we buy something we trust the shop to have put a real product in front of us, and we rarely think about it. The habits come from previous generations, from our own lives, and from the way language and culture grew up around the question of who to believe. Knowing who and what to trust, and who to avoid, is close to the basic survival skill, and it is how people stay alive and get on over time.

The other place trust lives is in institutions and certifications. Parents spend their lives trying to get a child into a trusted position: a lawyer, a doctor, a certified electrician. Those positions bring status and prosperity and the rest of what people want for the people they love, and every one of them rests on trust. This person is going to operate on my spouse, and that one is going to wire the house my child plays in. The certificate answers the question before a stranger has to ask it.

What AI breaks

One of the most consequential things AI does is break those habits. We learned to trust fluent prose, well organised documents, and software that runs, because producing any of them took a person thousands of hours. The output pointed at what we were really trusting: a person who had invested themselves, often carrying the investment of a family or a profession behind them, and had earned the freedom and the responsibility that let them perform feats of eloquence, organisation, and discipline.

AI makes those signals free. A model produces the prose, the document, and the running software in seconds, and none of it points at anybody. So all of your instincts about who and what to trust are suddenly wrong, at the same moment you are exposed to forces faster and more violent than anything the old habits were built for.

Security

Software security shows it first. Until recently only trained engineers could put together a working production system, and in the years it took to learn that, they also picked up the profession's habits around safety and security, the way an apprentice picks up the habits of a trade without anyone naming them. Now a person who understands nothing about software can build the same system in an afternoon, with none of the culture, tradition, or norms that used to arrive with the skill. The system works, and nobody in the room knows what they have left out.

Correctness

The same thing happens to correctness. A trainee accountant spends years learning the habits and norms of a profession built up over centuries, and is eventually certified. At that point they can build a financial model or close a set of accounts robustly, and because of everything invested in getting there, they have a strong reason to make sure the work is correct, thorough, and up to the standards of the craft.

A model produces the same financial model, except that no reputation stands behind it and nothing guarantees it is right. Humans make mistakes too, but a human can suffer for a mistake and knows it, and that is a large part of why careful work gets done. A model disappears the moment you have used it. It was never really there; it is electricity moving through metal wires.

The problem

So you get the appearance of eloquence, thought, analysis, and trustworthiness, with none of the substance behind it. At the same time nobody can pretend the tools are anything other than extremely useful. That is the difficult position everyone using them is in: how to begin working with tools that are this powerful and this dangerous when, by construction, they cannot be trusted and cannot be relied on to be right.
