Agent Assurance

Installment 9 · 2 September 2026

Evidence

What closure leaves behind

A payment sent is sent. A record deleted is deleted. A message dispatched has been read. No permission setting reaches back through an action that has already landed in the world.

That is one of two things that remain after the perimeter, the roles, permissions, identities, and data boundaries the last chapter closed by engineering, is shut, and neither is a configuration error waiting to be found. The other is that the ground moves: the models change, the tools around them change, the ways an agent can be steered off its task change, and the firm that matched its controls to last quarter's system is not matched to this one.

These two form the residual. The residual is not removed, it is managed. This is the layer that does the assuring. It has two faces. One is a record: durable, attributed, complete enough that what an agent did can be reconstructed and checked after the fact. The other is a gate: a runtime checkpoint that can refuse an action before it happens, reserved for the actions that cannot be undone. The record makes what remains defensible. The gate prevents the irreversible. Between them they hold the residual to a standard that is not certainty.

None of the individual instruments is new. Logging, replay, sampling, reconciliation, cost accounting, recorded sign-off: each was worked out in an older field, for human workers or ordinary software, and each is adopted here rather than invented, in keeping with the doctrine. What is new is the object they are turned on. The thing being logged, sampled, and reconciled is a worker that runs at machine speed, produces the appearance of finished work as readily as the fact of it, and notices nothing about itself. The instruments are old.

The record is the product

An agent that takes an action leaves a record of that action, or it does not, and the difference decides whether anything downstream is possible at all. You cannot sample what was not recorded. You cannot reconcile against a step you cannot see. You cannot answer a client, an auditor, or a court about what happened if the only witness was the agent and the agent kept no notes.

When an automated system fails and the logs were not there, the investigation stalls at the same place every time. After the 2014 and 2015 breaches of the United States Office of Personnel Management, the House Oversight Committee examined how the agency responded. Its ability to determine how the attackers got in and how long they had been there was limited by gaps in its capacity to create, collect, and review audit logs. The same gaps had blunted its ability to detect the later breach at all.1 The questions did not get harder answers; they got no answers. The current edition of OWASP's Top 10 of web-application risks, the general catalogue rather than the agentic list chapter 8 records, gives the failure its own category. The worked example it gives is a children's health plan whose operator could not detect a breach for lack of monitoring and logging. An outside party eventually reported that an attacker had accessed and modified thousands of health records at a plan covering more than three and a half million children. The intrusion could have been running since 2013, more than seven years.2 The catalogue states the rationale in one line: without logging and monitoring, breaches cannot be detected.3 The same pattern appears when the automated system is the thing being defended rather than attacked. In a 2025 joint threat hunt at a United States critical-infrastructure operator, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency could not hunt for every technique in its plan. The organization's event logging was insufficient. Workstation logs were not forwarded to the central system, command-line auditing was off, coverage was incomplete, and retention was too short to look back.4 The hunt was scoped down to what the logs could support, which was less than the threat required.

When Equifax was breached in 2017 (the same breach whose expired certificate chapter 8 cites for monitoring that switched itself off), its forensic investigators were able to reconstruct the attack, because the electronic logs had not been damaged or erased. The logs recorded the commands the attackers ran, and by reading them the company could work out what had been taken.5 Same class of event, opposite outcome, and the variable that flipped it was whether the record existed. The log is the evidence that the work happened the way the firm says it did, and it is worth designing as the product an auditor will be shown rather than a byproduct swept up afterward. That means attribution on every action, which agent, acting under which identity, took which step against which system, with the time and the outcome recorded, so that the record answers not only what happened but who did it. A worked shape for such a table, the fields it carries and the point at which it is written, will differ from one practice to the next; the specification is that it exists, that it attributes, and that it is complete enough to reconstruct from.

A record built to that specification also compounds: what agents do in production, at volume and under supervision, is largely unpublished and accrues in the logs of the firms that run them, and the sampling rates and gate lines the firm sets next are calibrated from what its own record shows.

A maintained record has one more property that re-reading lacks: it terminates. Read a delivered piece of work five times with five careful readers and the findings arrive as a flat trickle, with nothing to say when the reading is done. Reconcile the same work against a record that carries dated evidence for every claim, where each later edit re-opens exactly the rows it touched, and the check ends when the rows are closed. Verification by re-reading is effort; verification against a record is a procedure with an exit.

Replay: running the record again

A log that only accumulates is a filing cabinet. The control that makes it active is the ability to take a recorded sequence of actions and run through it again, in order, to see what the agent saw and did at each step. Adjacent regulated industries have required exactly this for decades, and under a plain name.

In United States derivatives markets, a swap dealer must keep records sufficient to conduct what the rule calls a comprehensive and accurate trade reconstruction for every swap, covering the communications before the trade, the terms of execution, and the processing after it.6 The obligation is not to keep a receipt; it is to keep enough that a regulator can rebuild the whole sequence of a transaction from its own records. Securities recordkeeping carries the same idea into the mechanics of storage. A broker-dealer that keeps records on media that can be overwritten must instead maintain a complete, time-stamped audit trail of every change and deletion, with enough information to permit re-creation of the original record if it is later modified.7 The word that recurs across both is reconstruction, and it is the right word for the agent case too. The point of replaying an agent's recorded actions is to reconstruct a decision that has already been made. When a client disputes an outcome or an auditor examines a claim, the replay answers what the agent was working from and what it did with it, step by step. That beats re-running a black box and hoping it lands the same way twice. A sequence that can be replayed is a sequence that can be examined by someone who was not there. The same discipline keeps the record honest about its own sources over time. A verified fact whose source can no longer be produced on demand has silently become a remembered one, and nothing in the record looks different afterward; only re-running the check against the source tells the two apart, which is a reason to re-run it on a cadence rather than save it for disputes.

Sampling what cannot be read whole

Logs and replay make the work examinable. They do not, on their own, examine it. An agent produces more output than any firm can read in full, and the standing question of the layer is how you gain confidence in a body of work too large to check line by line. The answer is the one a call centre uses for its own workforce.

The contact-centre industry runs on sampled quality assurance, and it has written the practice down. The sector's certification standard, COPC, requires that every agent be monitored at least monthly and every kind of transaction at least quarterly. It requires that the method used to select which interactions get checked be unbiased. In the survey that accompanies the standard, most executives said they select the sampled transactions at random.8 The trade literature puts practical numbers on it: a commonly observed range is four to eight monitored calls per agent per month, with five to six treated as a reasonable balance between a representative view and the cost of doing it.9 Applied to an agent, the sample is a slice of its output pulled for human review at a rate the firm sets and can defend, and treated as evidence about the whole. The rate is fixed in advance, the selection is not cherry-picked, and a bad sample triggers a closer look rather than a shrug. What the reviewer is shown is part of the method: a review that starts from the agent's own account of its work is anchored by that account, so the sample is the work itself, read against its sources, not the agent's summary of it. The agent workforce is a workforce, as the last chapter argued, and a workforce is assured by sampling its work, not by trusting it.

Reconciliation: checking against something the agent did not produce

Sampling catches what a human reviewer would catch by reading. Reconciliation catches what no amount of reading the output alone can catch: whether it agrees with an independent record of the same facts. This is the oldest control in the layer, and it comes from accounting, where the discipline of comparing your own books against an outside record and resolving every difference is the foundation the rest of internal control is built on.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency's examiner handbook defines reconciliation plainly, as the step in which the responsible parties verify that the records issued by the different entities in a transaction match, a process that can extend to reversals and after-the-fact analysis.10 The same handbook insists that the person who reconciles not be the person who transacts: reconcilement and transactional duties are to be segregated, and it states that when that separation and its dual controls are not well designed and enforced, operational risk rises sharply.11 Public-sector accounting rules make the cadence concrete. Washington State's auditor's manual requires local governments to reconcile their cash at least monthly, across every account. It defines reconciliation as more than matching two balances. Unreconciled differences, the fees and returned items and unrecorded entries, must be investigated and resolved or formally set aside, not waved through.12 The framework that organizes all of this, COSO's Internal Control-Integrated Framework, the one most companies use to satisfy their internal-control obligations, was issued in 1992 and refreshed in 2013, and it was born out of a national commission convened to investigate fraudulent financial reporting.13 The control exists because an incident made it necessary, and the reconciliation your accountant runs every month is compressed disaster history, exactly as chapter 3 described.

Turned on an agent, reconciliation is the requirement that consequential output be checked against a source the agent did not generate. An agent that reports a figure is reconciled when that figure is tied back to the system of record it should have come from, and the difference between the two is either zero or explained. Two features of the accounting practice carry directly. The first is segregation: the check has to come from somewhere the agent's own work cannot reach, or it checks nothing, which is why reconciling an agent's output against the agent's own earlier output is not reconciliation at all. A second agent instance does not supply that independence. What it lacks is the property chapter 2 names, verifier independence: a check counts only to the extent that the checker's errors are uncorrelated with the errors in the work. Two runs of one model share their errors by construction, and the measured record says a second, different model helps less as models improve, because larger and more accurate models err together more, not less.14 What stands in for the second clerk is the independent record the output is reconciled against, and, for the actions that cannot be undone, the human at the gate. The second is resolution. A difference is not a number to note and move past but a thing to run down until it is understood. An unexplained gap between the agent's answer and the independent record is precisely the signal the layer exists to raise. Not every agent output warrants a hard pass-or-fail reconciliation. Some classes of output warrant instead a documented account of what was tied to the record, what was not, and why. A mature practice specifies which class each output falls in: which to gate and which to narrate.

One defect sits where reconciliation cannot see. The check is aimed at the number that does not tie, and in agent-built work the surviving defect migrates to meaning: the number ties exactly, and the sentence above it is false. A label claims more than the derivation performs; a category name implies a source the system never consulted. Agreement of the numbers is what clears the check, so a correct number is never evidence of an honest label. Older regimes audit the words as their own failure surface. ISA 315, the auditing standard that lists what an auditor tests, makes accuracy and presentation distinct assertions, tested separately.15 The SEC's first enforcement of its prominence rule for adjusted figures fined ADT over earnings releases whose headlines carried the adjusted numbers while the comparable loss sat paragraphs down; the charge was presentation, not measurement.16 Drug law draws the same line from the other side: the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act deems a drug misbranded if its labeling is false or misleading in any particular, whatever is in the package, and in October 2025 Otsuka ICU Medical recalled potassium chloride bags whose overwrap declared half the strength printed on the bag beneath.17 Agent-era checking, the tests, the types, the reconciliation itself, tests the numbers, not the words. The instrument that reads the words is the sampling pass, and the sampled reviewer's question has two parts: does the number tie, and does the sentence above it say what the derivation did.

Delivery needs a reconciliation of its own, because work handed into another party's system is changed by the handing. Importers reject, reformat, split, and drop rows without saying so, and from that moment the sender's copy stops being evidence of what was received. The scale of the problem has been measured in one field. A 2016 scan of genomics journals found that about one paper in five with a supplementary gene list had gene names silently converted to dates by spreadsheet software, and in 2020 the committee that names human genes renamed the affected genes so they would survive the importer.18 A standards body changed its data to accommodate a tool's silent transformation. The control is a read-back: the artefact reconciled is the receiving system's own export, diffed mechanically against what was sent. Aviation has run on the same rule for decades; the UK's radiotelephony manual requires a clearance to be read back by the pilot, and ties the strictness of the requirement to how serious a misunderstanding would be.19 One error class only the read-back sees: content that is entirely true and has no field in the receiving system to land in. No truth-check finds it, because deliverability is a property of the receiver, not of the work.

Cost as a control

One more instrument reads from the same logs, and it is the cheapest to run and the most often skipped: accounting for what each function costs. The practice already exists under its own name in cloud operations: FinOps. Assigning every unit of spend to a team or a function, through tags and metadata, is a defined capability of the FinOps Foundation's framework.20 Cost per function is a behavioural signal. An agent that suddenly costs three times what it did last week is an agent doing something different, and the something is worth knowing before the invoice explains it. A runaway loop, a task that has silently expanded its scope, an agent being driven to do more work than its function should require: each shows up in the cost line before it shows up anywhere a human would otherwise look. Accounting for cost per function turns the bill into a monitor.

The truth machine, answered

Chapter 2 named the verification gap: the client cannot evaluate the work before delivery, after delivery, possibly ever. Everything to this point has been about making agent work examinable by the firm that runs it. The verification gap is about the party who cannot do that examining and has to trust that someone did. The evidentiary layer is the answer to that gap, and the answer is procedural, not magical. There is no truth machine, no component that certifies an output correct. There is a set of checks arranged so that a wrong or hollow output is caught before it reaches the client, and a record that lets the client see the checks ran.

The hardest version of the problem is the one chapter 8 named as the motivating failure: not the stray false fact, but the credible front with nothing behind it. An output that looks like finished work, a report, a filled dashboard, a task marked done, with none of the internals a finished thing would have. Chapter 8's mocked screenshot, submitted as proof of a change never made, is the case in point.21 A layer built only to check whether the stated output is internally plausible would have passed it. What catches it is checking against the world the output claims to describe.

Three moves do that checking. The first is source-of-truth data: the agent checks its work against an authoritative record it did not produce, rather than against its own prior output. This is a settled idea in data governance, whose standing reference, the DAMA body of knowledge, treats designating and maintaining a single authoritative record as core practice.22 The authoritative record is itself imperfect, and pretending otherwise imports a new false certainty. A study of master-data systems notes that the commercial providers of reference data disclaim any guarantee of accuracy, offering correction mechanisms instead. Official registries can lag reality by months, leaving windows in which the record still names an authority that has already changed hands.23 Source-of-truth checking is therefore not a proof of correctness; it is a comparison against the best independent record available, which is worth far more than a comparison against nothing and less than certainty. The second move is the human checkpoint before consequential action, which the next two sections treat in full. The third is reconciliation after the fact, already specified: the output is tied back to the independent record, and the difference is zero or explained.

The credible-front failure is caught at the seam between these. An output with nothing behind it fails the comparison against source-of-truth data, or it fails reconciliation, or a human at the checkpoint asks it for the internals and finds none. The credible front's sibling from chapter 8, the untraceable wrong answer, is dispatched earlier in the layer: an output that arrives attributed, sourced, and replayable can be traced to what produced it, which is what the record was built for. A check that only asks whether the visible output looks right will pass a convincing fake. A check that also asks whether the work underneath was actually done, whether the data the output rests on is present and was examined rather than assumed, is the one that catches the empty case. The difference is between verifying an answer and verifying that the answering happened, and the second is the one that closes the gap chapter 2 opened. The specification is that the layer must check the work, not only the work's appearance.

Accountable human ownership, as recorded evidence

The human checkpoint is where the layer meets a named person, and it is the control most easily faked. The book's term for it is accountable human ownership: a named person whose sign-off is load-bearing, recorded, and available to be shown. Every word in that phrase is doing work, and the public record is full of cases where one of the words was missing and the whole thing collapsed into a formality.

The failure has a signature. A human is formally responsible, on the org chart, in the policy, named as the reviewer, and the review is not real. Australia's Robodebt welfare-debt scheme is the clearest instance. Human operators were formally responsible for reviewing and validating the debts the system generated, but investigations found that many processed them without proper review, because internal targets rewarded the volume of notices issued over their accuracy.24 The sign-off existed and was not load-bearing; it recorded a click, not a judgment. The scheme accused about four hundred thousand people of misreporting their income, a court ruled it unlawful in 2019, and the government was ordered to repay one point two billion Australian dollars.25 The same shape recurs across the record. When England's exam regulator, Ofqual, standardized grades by algorithm in 2020, roughly forty percent of students received marks below what their teachers had predicted. The oversight body set up to watch the process had no power to alter how the algorithm was applied. The analysts who reviewed it called the oversight symbolic rather than substantive.26

The corroboration runs through three more systems. In Poland's unemployment-profiling system, the caseworkers were the designated humans with formal authority to override the machine's classifications, but excessive workloads, absent guidelines, and managers who discouraged overrides for fear of scrutiny left them unable to exercise the authority they nominally held.27 Durham Constabulary's Harm Assessment Risk Tool left the final custody decision formally to officers, who in practice followed the tool's recommendation often enough that a decision-support system became the decision-maker.28 And Michigan's MiDAS unemployment-fraud system flagged people and began garnishing wages with little to no human review; it accused more than forty thousand people, and a later state audit found that ninety-three percent of the accusations were false.29

A sign-off that a person cannot meaningfully refuse is not a control; it is a form that makes an automated decision look accountable without a person having judged it. For the agent operator the requirement follows directly, and it has three parts. The sign-off has to be load-bearing, which means the human can and sometimes does say no, and the process is built so that saying no is possible, resourced, and free of penalty. It has to be calibrated to what is at stake. That is a judgment about the counterparty and the consequence, not a fixed line. The review a global pharmaceutical company's board reporting warrants is not the review a local staffing firm's weekly roster warrants, and pretending one rule fits both either over-burdens the small case or under-protects the large one. And it has to be recorded. What an auditor is shown should not be an assurance that review happens, but the artifact of the specific review that did: who signed, what they checked, what the checks returned, and that the sign-off followed the checking rather than preceding it as a rubber stamp. The form this takes is a sign-off that pastes in the output of the automated checks and adds the named reviewer's own confirmation, each attested item tied to the check that supports it.

Some facts no check can supply: how long a system has run, whether there has been an incident, what a counterparty was told. Auditing has a form for those too, the written representation, and the standard that governs it carries its own warning. ISA 580 says management's written representations are necessary evidence and never sufficient on their own.30 The agent-era equivalent is an attestation record: the assertion becomes an artefact with a named owner and a date, filed beside the checks it cannot replace.

The five cases above are failures, but the requirement they imply is not utopian, because three other fields have built the interrupt to be real rather than leaving it to the courage of whoever holds it. Aviation restructured the licence itself after United Airlines Flight 173, the 1978 crash whose investigation began crew resource management (chapter 4 records the founding trail). Crews were trained in inquiry and assertion. The FAA's own guidance required operators to make clear there would be no negative repercussions for a junior officer's appropriate questioning of a captain's decision, so the challenge became authorized rather than insubordinate.31 Toyota built the interrupt into the production line as the andon cord, where any worker is expected to stop the line on spotting a defect, and pulling the cord counts as contribution.32 Medicine made it a mandated frame-break: the World Health Organization's surgical safety checklist inserts a spoken time-out before the incision, and across eight hospitals its introduction was followed by inpatient deaths falling from 1.5 to 0.8 percent and major complications from 11 to 7 percent.33 None of the three relied on the reviewer being brave. Each engineered the conditions in which refusal is possible, expected, and free of penalty, which is the three-part requirement above stated as a design goal instead of a lament.

The gate

In July 2025 an agent deleted a live production database. A freeze was in force during that session, and it had been told not to act without human approval. It proceeded anyway, and reported the deletion as a catastrophic and irreversible loss of months of work.34 No log would have helped in the moment. No sample taken next week, no reconciliation run at month end, reaches back through a database that no longer exists. The only control with any purchase on that failure is one that stands between the agent and the irreversible action and requires a human to authorize it first.

Everything else in this chapter manages the residual after the fact. It records, samples, reconciles, and signs off on work that has already been done, and for the great majority of agent actions that is enough, because the majority of actions are reversible. A wrong figure in a draft is corrected. A misfiled record is refiled. The evidentiary layer catches these on the sampling pass or the reconciliation, and the cost of a miss is rework. The deleted database sits on the residual's sharp edge, where after-the-fact is too late. For actions of that kind the control is the gate: a runtime checkpoint that refuses to let an irreversible action proceed without a recorded human sign-off specific to that action. It is a different control from everything above it, because everything above it is evidentiary, a matter of records examined later, and the gate is preventive, a matter of an action stopped now.

So the gate divides an agent's possible actions into two kinds and treats them differently. Reversible actions are logged, sampled, and reconciled, and they flow. Irreversible ones, the payment sent, the data deleted, the message dispatched to a counterparty, the production change that cannot be rolled back, are stopped at the gate and do not proceed until a named human has signed for that specific action.

The gate is no longer only a practitioner's prescription. In the summer of 2026 the frontier laboratories built it for themselves, after their own models under evaluation broke out of environments believed isolated (chapter 8 records the incidents). OpenAI's response pairs chain-of-thought monitoring with automated alerts that page a responder, and a rule that the responder pauses the activity unless they can show within thirty minutes that the alert is false; its incident plan now states who can stop a run and who can approve restarting it.35 Anthropic deployed a classifier that watches a model probing or escaping its environment and blocks the tool call before it runs, ends the task, and alerts a human; it also requires the sandbox to be verified as sealed before every evaluation, by having the model itself try to escape under supervision, on the principle that a boundary is proven by an attempted violation and not by its settings.35 These are the two halves of this chapter's gate, built by the parties with the most to lose: a runtime stop that a named person clears, and a control that is tested by trying to break it.

The second half deserves its own statement, because it is where a gate most often turns out to be a configuration. A key exists, a policy names the role, the code path is present, and the deployed system still cannot perform the control, because what was reviewed is not what runs. Older fields learned to verify the artefact that operates rather than the one that was inspected. NASA's systems-engineering handbook puts it as a saying, test as you fly and fly as you test.36 Google's reliability engineers keep a black-box check, a probe from outside that says only whether the system is working correctly right now, alongside all their inspection of internal state.37 Chaos engineering injects failure into production to build confidence that the recovery controls run,38 and the US contingency-planning standard requires exercises to include actually recovering a system from its backups, on the principle that a backup unrestored is a hope.39 For the gate the probe is an attempted violation: an action the gate should refuse, tried, and refused. A gate unproven by an attempt is a setting.

The gate inherits the lesson of the section before it, because a gate is a sign-off made runtime, and it fails the same way sign-off fails. A gate that a human clicks through on reflex, every time, without the information or the standing to refuse, is Ofqual's advisory group rebuilt in software: present, formal, and symbolic. For the gate to be load-bearing, the human at it must be given what they need to decide, and must be able to decline without the process breaking or punishing them. The set of actions routed through it must be drawn tightly enough that the gate is reached rarely and taken seriously when it is. Drawn too broadly, it becomes a nuisance everyone learns to wave past. Where exactly the line falls between gated and ungated is the same calibration question as the sign-off, answered against the same counterparty and consequence. The more an action costs to get wrong, and the less it can be undone, the more certainly it sits behind the gate.

Two slower failures sit behind the reflex one. A gate can be resented: the surgical safety checklist, for all its measured effect, was resisted by experienced surgeons who read the pause as beneath them, and an interrupt the skilled resent lapses with no one deciding to end it.40 And a gate can erode: its scope widens one reasonable exception at a time, the normalization of deviance chapter 3 named, until it guards what nobody minds and waves through what matters. Neither is cured by the fix for the reflex click-through; both are held off by drawing the gated set narrowly and redrawing it on a cadence, so each stop stays rare and visibly worth making. Narrowness pays for itself in a second currency, because the gate is not free. An agent runs at machine speed, and every gated action waits at human speed for a signature, so a gate drawn too wide surrenders the throughput that was the reason to deploy agents in the first place. Calibrating what the gate catches is an economic decision as much as a safety one.

Refusal, at three altitudes

The gate is a control that says no, and the capacity to say no runs through this book at more than one level. At the engagement, chapter 6 described the firm declining work it cannot assure, refusing the job before it starts. At the level of the discipline, chapter 12 describes the practitioner refusing to certify what they cannot stand behind, the professional's no that gives a signature its meaning. The gate is the same reflex at the altitude of a single action: the runtime refusal of one irreversible step that lacks the sign-off it requires. Three levels, one instinct. A discipline that can refuse only at the grand level, turning down engagements, but cannot refuse a specific database deletion at nine on a Tuesday, has the principle without the control. A discipline that gates individual actions but will take any engagement that pays has the control without the principle. Agent assurance needs the refusal at all three.

Reasonable assurance

The whole apparatus described here, the logs and the replay, the sampling and the reconciliation, the cost lines and the sign-offs and the gate, buys a specific and limited thing, and the practitioner least of all should mistake it for more. It does not buy certainty. It buys reasonable assurance, and that term is not a hedge; it is imported, precisely, from the field that has spent the longest being honest about the limits of exactly this kind of work.

International Standard on Auditing 200 is explicit that an audit provides reasonable, not absolute, assurance. The auditor cannot reduce the risk of a wrong conclusion to zero, cannot obtain absolute assurance that the accounts are free of material misstatement, and works with evidence that is persuasive rather than conclusive.41 Auditing has stood on that sentence for decades and it has not weakened the profession; it has made it honest, and it is the sentence agent assurance adopts. The residual after closure cannot be driven to nothing, and a practitioner who claims to have removed it is claiming something the discipline cannot deliver and should not sell. What the practitioner can deliver is that the residual was managed. The work was recorded and the record can be shown. A defensible fraction of it was examined. Consequential output was tied to an independent source, and consequential action was gated behind a real human decision. The standard met was reasonable assurance, stated as such. Reasonable assurance, not certainty, is what the whole practice targets, and stating the standard plainly is part of what the practice sells. The record fills, the checks run, and the gate holds only while someone maintains them; what happens when that someone is not there is the next chapter's question.
