Chapter 5: The Economics of the Control Layer

Price

It is natural to think this is early, that it will mature, and that the checking will get cheaper along with everything else. The economics run the other way.

The complements law

Two goods are complements when using more of one makes the other more valuable.1 Cheap computers raised demand for printers, monitors, and internet access.1 When a good gets cheap, the money moves to whatever is used beside it and is still scarce.

Carl Shapiro and Hal Varian said it of technology markets in Information Rules in 1999: "Technology changes. Economic laws do not."2

The good that is collapsing in price

The good getting cheap is machine intelligence, priced by the token. Gartner estimates that by 2030 a query to a one-trillion-parameter model will cost providers more than ninety percent less than in 2025, up to a hundred times cheaper than the first models of that size, built in 2022.3

Cheaper per token is not cheaper in total. Agents use five to thirty times more tokens per task than a chatbot, and falling provider costs will not all reach the customer.4

In April 2026 the Pragmatic Engineer newsletter reported token spend up roughly tenfold in six months at two companies, and one seed-stage AI infrastructure firm's spend per developer up from about 200 dollars a month to about 3,000.5 Cheaper intelligence means more of it gets used, so more work reaches the world that nobody has checked. That is where the money for checking comes from.

What stays scarce

Three things stay scarce as intelligence gets cheap: accountability, verified context, and standing. Accountability is a named person on the hook when the work is wrong. Verified context is information that was checked against a source. Standing is a sign-off a bank, an insurer, or a regulator will accept. A model can fake all three for nothing, and the fake is worth nothing.

Judgment looks cheap too now. A model can write the audit memo and the confident sign-off. The best-known account of AI economics, from Agrawal, Gans, and Goldfarb, had said judgment would stay the expensive part while prediction went free, and a 2026 paper in the economics of science says that stopped holding once models could produce competent-looking judgment for nothing.6

What looks like judgment can be produced with no judgment behind it. In people the two came together, so we learned to read one from the other, and a model breaks that. Judgment is the part with consequence attached: a person whose name is on the memo and who bears what follows if it is wrong. The same paper names what stays scarce as verified signal, legitimacy, and provenance; for agents that is verified context, standing, and the person accountable.6 The look of judgment gets cheap, and the consequence stays where it was, with a person.

Being right stays valuable, a name on the work stays valuable, and both are worth more where the work is regulated or runs inside an organization. Three 2026 preprints reach that from different directions. The first finds durable value in professional sign-off, regulated workflows, and evidence trails.7 The second finds that splitting work into cheap swappable parts pays less as verifying them gets dearer.8 The third puts value in what a model cannot copy, an organization's own data and routines among them.9 None of the three is about agent assurance.

Insurers have started to price this. In 2025 W. R. Berkley wrote what one coverage analysis called the first absolute AI exclusion into three of its liability lines: no claim arising from the use, deployment, or development of artificial intelligence by anyone.10 Specialists went the other way: the first agent policy backed by a certification standard went live in February 2026, and one structure pairs its AI limits with model certification and compliance reporting.11 The market is sorting agent risk into its own class.

The market of one

Most business logic has never been worth custom software. Bespoke software costs the same to build however few people use it, and for one firm's way of doing one thing that cost almost always exceeded what the firm could justify. Software for one customer's one workflow is a market of one, and it was never viable while developer time set the price.

An OECD survey in 2024 found one in four small and medium firms citing cost as a reason not to adopt digital tools at all, about as many unable to see the benefit, and the firms furthest behind saying they did not know where to start.12 A 2020 synthesis of 132 studies found the same barriers, eighteen of them, cost and in-house expertise recurring throughout.13 The software they wanted cost more than it was worth, so it was never built.

Those firms used spreadsheets instead. A 2023 study of 21,398 United States job postings found spreadsheet skills required in about half.14 Business runs on spreadsheets wherever custom software was never affordable.

An audit of fifty operational spreadsheets, reported in 2008, found errors in ninety-four percent of them.15 The system that carries the business carries defects no one has looked for, and was never designed, reviewed, or handed over. The same number measures demand: ninety-four percent were broken and the businesses depended on them anyway, so a flawed answer beats no answer.

The line moves

Whether building software has got cheap enough to make the market of one viable is measured most recently in early 2026, and the measure ages fast. By January 2026 the leading agents finished tasks of roughly five hours of human work about half the time, and that horizon had been doubling about every three months.16 The last controlled trial of developers with agentic tools, in early 2026, found no slowdown, and up to half of the developers invited refused to do some of their work without the tools.17

The cheapest software to build is the new kind, described from scratch and built once, which is the market of one. Checking is needed even there: in the trials so far the developers could not tell whether the tool had made them faster, and a market cannot price a tool by results nobody can measure.1817

The backlog also grows from the other end. A process that ran in someone's head or over the phone can now, with an afternoon and a chatbot, run in a spreadsheet or a script. No survey measures it yet. Each new one is demand for something built properly.

The shadow artifact is the requirements document

The improvised spreadsheet, the unsanctioned tool, the document the whole team depends on and no one owns: each is a shadow artifact, and the most accurate requirements document the client will ever produce. Someone with the problem built the smallest thing that solved it, kept it because it worked, and changed it whenever reality changed, for years. A fresh requirements document would be a guess about the work, and the spreadsheet is the work.

The error rate is part of the signal: it marks the artifact as load-bearing and unsafe at once. The engagement starts from the shadow artifact, keeps the workflow it encodes, and rebuilds it on ground that can be inspected and stood behind.

Why the work lasts

The work lasts because the risk is irreducible. A problem that gets solved becomes a cheap tool and its assurance evaporates. This one does not get solved: after every control there is still an ecosystem of models, tools, and attacks that keeps changing, and actions that reach the world and cannot be recalled. The cheaper the agents, the more of them a business runs, and the more work reaches the world unverified.
