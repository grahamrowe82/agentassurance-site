The Artifact and the Work

Cheap intelligence

Machine intelligence is getting cheaper every month, and it is hard to see where that stops. The natural expectation is that knowledge work gets easier with it: the emails, the decks, the reports, and the software all get faster and cheaper to make. That part has happened, and knowledge work has not got easier; and the reasons are strange enough to be worth a chapter.

The artifact and the work

Start with what knowledge work is. The email, the slide deck, the report, and even the software look like the work, because they are what gets sent, presented, and shipped. They are what the work leaves behind. The work itself is a person coming to understand something, usually by dealing with the real world for long enough that the understanding holds, and then writing it down.

AI makes the leftovers cheap. A model will produce the email, the deck, the report, and working software in minutes, fluent and well structured, whether or not anyone understood anything on the way. So the artifact has stopped being evidence that the work was done. When the real thing and the fake look the same at the point of sale you have a lemon market, and lemon markets go one way: the fakes crowd out the real, and real knowledge finds it harder to accumulate because nobody can tell where it is.

What a model does

A model does one thing extremely well. It turns one expression of knowledge into another, faithfully: notes into a memo, a memo into a plan, a description of a process into code that runs the process. What comes out contains what went in, in a different form, and nothing more.

That is why the same tool is useless on one job and transformative on the next. Ask it a question nobody has answered and you get a wall of text shaped like an answer. Give it knowledge somebody has already earned and it converts that knowledge into a better form, quickly and reliably, provided the people driving it know good engineering practice and know how to work the tools.

The spreadsheet

The clearest case I know is the spreadsheet a business runs on. Most businesses run on a few of them: the one that prices the jobs, the one that tracks the stock, the one that says who is owed what. Each was built by someone who had the problem, kept because it worked, and changed whenever the business changed, for years. Nobody designed it, nobody reviewed it, and nobody else dares touch it. It holds more true knowledge about how that business runs than any document the firm could write.

That spreadsheet is the input a model is good at. The knowledge in it is real, tested against the world every month, and already written down. Turning it into proper software, with a database, validation, permissions, and a screen anyone can use, converts one expression of the knowledge into another. In 2026 that can be done reliably, at a price that would have been unthinkable two years ago. No new knowledge is invented, because the business already knew how it worked.

This has already happened: you can talk a working piece of software into existence in an afternoon. Whether it is correct, and whether it is safe, are different questions.

The businesses I see are ready for it. They run on spreadsheets and no-code tools glued together over years, the people who built them are tired, and they want out. The way out is now cheap, and the transition is where the danger is.

Where the danger is

The danger comes from scale. A wrong spreadsheet is wrong in one cell, on one screen, in front of the person who built it and half knows its faults. Software converted from it runs the same logic on every record, every night, for everyone, and nobody has half-known it for years. The tools that make the conversion cheap make its consequences bigger and harder to see. Two questions nobody asked about a spreadsheet now have to be answered with evidence: whether it is correct, and whether it is safe.

Most people driving these tools do not yet understand the implications of working at this power and scale with their own information. The habits that usually arrive bundled with a new capability arrived late with this one, and the people using it are working in good faith without them.

Insurers have started to price this. In 2025 W. R. Berkley wrote what coverage lawyers called the first absolute AI exclusion into three of its liability lines: no claim arising from the use, deployment, or development of artificial intelligence by anyone. Specialists went the other way. The first agent policy backed by a certification standard went live in February 2026, and at least one structure ties its AI limits to model certification and compliance reporting. The market is sorting agent risk into its own class.

What the human is for

The human's job has moved. It used to be typing the email and building the deck. Now it is the harder thing behind them, working with a tool that contains knowledge and cannot create it. The people who use one well treat it as a way of reaching knowledge that already exists, earned by contact with the real world, and moving it into forms where more people can use it safely. What stays with the person is what always did: whether the thing is true, whether it works, and who answers when it fails.

Walls of text

By late 2026 it is clear that a great deal of what looked like new knowledge over the past two years was multiplication of words. A model produces reasoning-shaped prose without limit, it reads well, and it is causing real stress to the people who have to read it, because they cannot tell what was thought and what was generated. Real knowledge is practical and embedded, in David Deutsch's sense of the word: what someone knows because they did the thing and it worked. Words are a report of it, and a report can be written by anyone.

So the checking gets no cheaper as the intelligence does. The artifact is what gets cheap, while the knowledge behind it, and the person who answers for it, cost what they always cost. And every cheap artifact is one more thing somebody has to check.
