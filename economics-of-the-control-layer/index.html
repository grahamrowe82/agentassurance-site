Chapter 5: The Economics of the Control Layer

Price

It is natural to think this is early, that it will mature, and that the checking will get cheaper along with everything else. The economics run the other way.

The complements law

Two goods are complements when using more of one makes the other more valuable.1 Cheap computers raised demand for printers, monitors, and internet access.1 When a good gets cheap, the money moves to whatever is used beside it and is still scarce.

Carl Shapiro and Hal Varian said it of technology markets in Information Rules in 1999: "Technology changes. Economic laws do not."2

The good that is collapsing in price

The good getting cheap is machine intelligence, priced by the token. Gartner estimates that by 2030 a query to a one-trillion-parameter model will cost providers more than ninety percent less than in 2025, up to a hundred times cheaper than the first models of that size, built in 2022.3

Cheaper per token is not cheaper in total. Agents use five to thirty times more tokens per task than a chatbot, and falling provider costs will not all reach the customer.4

In April 2026 the Pragmatic Engineer newsletter reported token spend up roughly tenfold in six months at two companies, and one seed-stage AI infrastructure firm's spend per developer up from about 200 dollars a month to about 3,000.5 Bills rise while the unit price falls, as they did for bandwidth and storage. That is how a complement behaves.

What stays scarce

Three things stay scarce as intelligence gets cheap: accountability, verified context, and standing. Accountability is a named person on the hook when the work is wrong. Verified context is information that was checked against a source. Standing is a sign-off a bank, an insurer, or a regulator will accept. A model can fake all three for nothing, and the fake is worth nothing.

If a model can write the audit memo and produce the confident sign-off, judgment looks cheap too. Agrawal, Gans, and Goldfarb say AI makes prediction free while judgment stays expensive.6 A 2026 paper in the economics of science says that stops holding once models produce competent-looking judgment at near-zero cost.6

What stays scarce is what the fake cannot supply. For science the paper names verified signal, legitimacy, and authentic provenance; for agents that is verified context and standing, with accountability underneath.6 A model can write a memo that reads like accountability, but it cannot be the person whose name is on it. The performance gets cheap, and the part a counterparty can test, hold, and sue stays scarce.

Other economists land near the same place. A 2026 paper on vertical AI markets finds durable value in professional sign-off, regulated workflows, and evidence trails.7 A second finds that breaking work into cheap swappable parts pays less as verifying the output gets dearer.8 A third lists proprietary data, computational infrastructure, distribution, and organizational routines.9 All three are preprints, and none is about agent assurance.

Insurers have started to price this. In 2025 W. R. Berkley wrote what one coverage analysis called the first absolute AI exclusion into three of its liability lines: no claim arising from the use, deployment, or development of artificial intelligence by anyone.10 Specialists went the other way: the first agent policy backed by a certification standard went live in February 2026, and one structure pairs its AI limits with model certification and compliance reporting.11 The market is sorting agent risk into its own class.

The market of one

Most business logic has never been worth custom software. Bespoke software costs the same to build however few people use it, and for one firm's way of doing one thing that cost almost always exceeded what the firm could justify. Software for one customer's one workflow is a market of one, and it was never viable while developer time set the price.

An OECD survey in 2024 found one in four small and medium firms citing cost as a reason not to adopt digital tools at all, about as many unable to see the benefit, and the firms furthest behind saying they did not know where to start.12 A 2020 synthesis of 132 studies found the same barriers, eighteen of them, cost and in-house expertise recurring throughout.13 The software they wanted cost more than it was worth, so it was never built.

Those firms used spreadsheets instead. When the Spreadsheet Engineering Research Project at Dartmouth's Tuck School surveyed 846 business-school alumni, published in 2005, seventy-nine percent called spreadsheets very important or critical to their job, and fewer than one in ten of their organizations had written standards for building them.14 A 2023 study of 21,398 United States job postings found spreadsheet skills required in about half.15 Business runs on spreadsheets wherever custom software was never affordable.

An audit of fifty operational spreadsheets, reported in 2008, found errors in ninety-four percent of them.16 The system that carries the business carries defects no one has looked for, and was never designed, reviewed, or handed over. The same number measures demand: ninety-four percent were broken and the businesses depended on them anyway, so a flawed answer beats no answer.

The line moves

If building software gets cheap enough, the market of one becomes viable and the backlog living in broken spreadsheets becomes software worth building. The best measure of whether that has happened is what agents can now do.

METR scores frontier agents by the length of software task they complete unassisted, in the time a skilled human would take. By January 2026 the leading model finished tasks of roughly five hours of human work about half the time, and that horizon had been doubling about every three months since 2024, against six to seven months across the record since 2019. The intervals are wide, and the longest tasks rest on estimated human baselines.17

The productivity experiments started earlier, on weaker tools. In 2023 and 2024, controlled studies found speedups of roughly twenty-one percent and fifty-six percent on defined tasks, with wide intervals.1819 A pooled analysis of three field experiments found about twenty-six percent more tasks completed, most among junior developers, and McKinsey put the effect at twenty to forty-five percent of software engineering spend.2021 A Google study found no significant change in developer telemetry, though developers liked the tool more.22

In mid-2025 METR ran a randomized trial with sixteen experienced open-source developers on codebases they had spent an average of five years inside. With AI tools they took nineteen percent longer. They had forecast a twenty-four percent speedup, and afterwards still estimated the tools had sped them up by twenty percent.23

In early 2026 METR re-ran it with new developers and the agentic tools that had arrived since. The slowdown was gone: four percent slower, inside an interval that straddled zero. Thirty to fifty percent of invited developers declined to submit tasks they would have to do without AI, so METR is redesigning the study. Up to half of them now refuse to do some of their work without the tools.24

If a senior engineer cannot tell whether the tool made them faster on their own code, the market cannot price the tool by results either. Checking does not commoditize, because it is needed even to know whether the cheap thing worked.

The build cost of software has fallen furthest where the work is new, described from scratch, and built once, which is the market of one. The one measured slowdown was deep inside mature systems, and on the latest measurement even that has faded to noise.

The backlog also grows from the other end. A process that ran in someone's head or over the phone can now, with an afternoon and a chatbot, run in a spreadsheet or a script. No survey measures it yet. Each new one is demand for something built properly.

The shadow artifact is the requirements document

The improvised spreadsheet, the unsanctioned tool, the document the whole team depends on and no one owns: each is a shadow artifact, and the most accurate requirements document the client will ever produce. Someone with the problem built the smallest thing that solved it, kept it because it worked, and changed it whenever reality changed, for years. A fresh requirements document would be a guess about the work, and the spreadsheet is the work.

The error rate is part of the signal: it marks the artifact as load-bearing and unsafe at once. The engagement starts from the shadow artifact, keeps the workflow it encodes, and rebuilds it on ground that can be inspected and stood behind.

Why the work lasts

The work lasts because the risk is irreducible. A problem that gets solved becomes a cheap tool and its assurance evaporates. This one does not get solved: after every control there is still an ecosystem of models, tools, and attacks that keeps changing, and actions that reach the world and cannot be recalled. The cheaper the agents, the more of them a business runs, and the more work reaches the world unverified.
