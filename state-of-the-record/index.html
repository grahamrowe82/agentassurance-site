Agent Assurance

Installment 11 · 16 September 2026

What Has Gone Wrong So Far

What the world has written down

Every chapter before this one has leaned on the public record. Incidents were chosen because they were documented, controls were traced to the failures that wrote them, and the whole method rests on the idea that a discipline is built from what has been recorded and can be checked. That raises a question the book has so far deferred: what does the public record of agent incidents actually contain? Who keeps it, how is it kept, and how much of what happens reaches it?

This chapter is the answer as of the date on it. The record is early, and the chapter will age faster than the others; every count here is stamped with the date it was taken, and the argument is built on the parts that will not move, which are the incentives that shape what gets written down. To answer the question at all, the author built an instrument: a coded corpus of every incident in the two working public registers, plus a smaller set of incidents traced to primary sources and read closely. The numbers below are that instrument's readings, and the chapter says so wherever it uses one. They are the author's own. What they measure is the record, not the world, and the difference between those two things is most of what this chapter has to say.

What exists

Two general-purpose registers of AI incidents are maintained and open. The AI Incident Database, a project of the Responsible AI Collaborative, indexes harms and near harms from deployed AI systems; the AIAAIC Repository describes itself as an independent, grassroots collection of incidents and ethical controversies.1 When the corpus was taken on 21 August 2026 it held 3,530 incidents from the two: 1,569 attributed to the first and 1,961 to the second.2 Both are built the same way. Someone reads the news, decides an item counts, and files it. Neither has a field that says whether the system involved was an agent, in the sense this book uses, a system that takes actions rather than producing output for a person to act on. Around them sit other collections that a reader searching for the agent record will find. The OECD's AI Incidents Monitor is an automated scan of news coverage, classified by machine, showing some seventeen thousand incidents and hazards.3 The AI Vulnerability Database is a knowledge base of failure modes whose stated scope now names agents.4 None of these is a register of agent incidents with the fields an operator or an insurer would need.

The absence claim has to be dated, so here it is dated: as of 21 August 2026, nobody publicly maintains a structured, growing collection of AI-agent incidents at any useful scale, and the test applied was concrete, an accessible, maintained source with at least a couple of hundred curated agentic incidents carrying structured coding. Nothing met it. That is not a criticism of the people running the registers. They built what their purpose required, and their purpose was accountability for harm, not frequency by control class. But it means the reader who asks "how often do agents fail, and how" cannot look it up. The rest of this chapter is what can be said instead.

What proper coding reveals

Reading the record is not the same as coding it. The author's corpus coded all 3,530 register incidents against a versioned taxonomy that asks, of each one, a small set of questions with fixed answers: was this a system failing, or a person using a working system to do harm; did the system act, or advise; if it failed, which control failed; how bad was the realised harm. The coding was done in batches by a single coder and then checked by a blind double-coding of twenty hard cases, and the results of that check travel with every number below, because a number without its reliability is a guess with a decimal point.5

The first finding is that a third of the "AI incident" record is not AI failing but AI being used as a weapon. Of the 3,530 incidents, 55 percent are system failures. Thirty-two percent are deliberate misuse: a person using an AI system as a weapon, the system working exactly as its operator intended, a deepfake made to defraud, a model used to write a scam at scale. Another 8 percent are human misuse of a working system, and 5 percent are contested.6 This matters because the two kinds need opposite controls. A system failure is answered by the controls in Part III of this book. Deliberate misuse is answered by the controls that answer any external attacker, and the honest operator reading a headline count of "AI incidents" is being told about both at once. The book's subject is the first kind, and the record, read plainly, says the first kind is about half of what gets filed.

The second finding is that a fifth of the record is not codeable incident data at all. About 14 percent of the register entries are real incidents in failure classes that predate agents entirely: biased classifiers, ordinary software defects, model performance below spec, a dataset that leaked personal data. Six percent are not incidents: lawsuits about business practices, research studies, controversies, a company's decision to do something. One percent could not be assessed for want of evidence.7 Even the 79 percent that remains is an upper bound, because the blind check found the batch coding too reluctant to refuse an entry, and every disagreement on that question ran the same way. The registers are, read as a whole, a record of the pre-agentic era of AI harm, assembled by people watching the news. They are a true record of what the news noticed.

The third finding is the one the book most needs and can least lean on. Fully agentic incidents, where the system took the action itself, are 6 percent of the record: 217 entries, and that figure is also an upper bound, because the blind check found the batch coding too willing to call a system agentic.8 Of the 217, the largest share is embodied autonomy, vehicles and robots, where the actuator is a steering wheel. Sixty-five are software agents of the kind this book is about. Sixty-five, in the entire public register record of AI harm, as of the summer of 2026. Where those sixty-five sit tells you about reporting rather than deployment: 31 in technology, 7 in retail, 6 in media, 3 in financial services, 1 in healthcare.9 The sectors with the most to lose from an agent acting wrongly are the sectors with the fewest public entries, which is what you would expect if firms in those sectors kept their incidents to themselves.

Two more readings, with their caveats attached. Among the system failures the corpus could code to a failure mode, two modes carry 90 percent of the primary causal weight: the agent acted beyond what it should have been allowed to do, and the agent acted on something it believed that was not true. No other mode reaches 3 percent.10 The reader of chapters 8 and 9 will recognise both: the first is what permission scoping and the gate exist for, the second is what reconciliation exists for. The caveat is that the long tail, prompt injection, tool misuse, a delegation that went wrong, is exactly what a register built from news cannot see, so its thinness in this corpus is not evidence of its rarity. And fully agentic failures skew severe: the most serious tier of realised harm is about 15 percent of fully agentic system failures against about 10 percent of the rest, a ratio of roughly one and a half.11 Part of that is the reporting itself, since a death is always recorded and an embarrassment is not; the direction is consistent with the plain expectation that a system which can act can do more damage than one which can only advise.

Why the seat is empty

Nothing in the previous section is difficult. The coding took one person a summer. The question is why it had not been done, and the answer is not neglect. It is that no one whose job it is to keep such a record has a reason to keep it publicly.

Start with who needs the data. Insurers need it: frequency and severity by control class is the raw material of pricing, and an underwriter who had it would price agent risk better than one who did not. Insurers therefore treat claims data as the most proprietary thing they own. The people who most need the record are the people least likely to publish it. Now turn to who does publish. The registers are run by policy and accountability communities, who code harm categories for advocacy, because that is what they are for. Security researchers publish the vulnerabilities they find in shipped agent products, and are paid in reputation for the disclosure, not for maintaining a database of everyone else's. Vendors and the frontier laboratories publish threat reports and system cards, which contain real dated events, but from the vendor's seat: aggregated, selected, and never carrying a deployer's name or loss. Each of these parties publishes what its purpose requires and stops.

Then there is the nature of the work. A register is a weekly discipline, not a paper or a product launch. Someone has to read the week's disclosures, decide what counts, code it against a scheme, merge the duplicates, and do it again next week, forever. Nothing in anyone's business model requires doing that in public. The coding also needs an odd combination of skills, security literacy to read the disclosure, an operator's sense of which control failed, and an insurer's habit of anchoring severity to realised loss, and each of the communities above has one of the three.

And the field is young. Cybersecurity offers the closest precedent. Public breach-notification laws began in 2003; Verizon's first Data Breach Investigations Report arrived in 2008; the open, community-coded database of breaches that anyone could query came in 2013.12 Registers lag their domains by a decade when the domain is well funded and the incidents are legally reportable. The agentic era is two years old and its incidents are, for the most part, not reportable to anyone. The seat is empty because the incentives that would fill it have not arrived, and the book states this as a description of the field rather than a complaint about it. It is the ordinary condition of a discipline whose incidents have started before its record-keeping.

Where the record actually lives

The strongest objection to everything above is that the laboratories write about agent incidents constantly. Anthropic and OpenAI publish periodic reports of the misuse they have detected and interrupted, with case studies;13 Anthropic says its system cards had already described the alignment issues behind its July incidents;14 security researchers publish the vulnerabilities they find in shipped agent products as they find them.15 Surely the record exists, and the registers are simply the wrong place to look.

The objection is right about the facts and wrong about the conclusion. The record is not absent. It is fragmented across channels, each shaped by the incentives of whoever keeps it, and each seeing only what pays it to look. Five channels carry nearly all of it.

The registers see what reached the press: deaths, lawsuits, and controversies, which is why they hold a pre-agentic record, since the harms that make news are the harms that were already happening before agents.16 Security research sees hazards in shipped products, and publishes them as blog posts and vulnerability numbers, one at a time, in forms that cannot be merged into a count of anything. Vendor and laboratory threat intelligence sees misuse of the vendor's own platform, and publishes it aggregated and selected, with the attacker's behaviour described and the victims unnamed. Courts see the litigated tail, which is small, slow, and the only channel that puts a number on the loss. And the deployer's own operations, the channel that holds the unauthorised action, the misdirected output, and the runaway spend, the kind of failure every operator of agents can name and none reports, reach no public channel at all, because nothing requires the deployer to tell anyone and every incentive runs the other way.

The consequence is that the two things a reader most wants, a count and a cost, are held by the channels least likely to publish them. The register can tell you how many incidents made the news. Security research can tell you how many ways a product can be attacked. The laboratories can tell you what their own models did under test. None of them can tell you how often an agent in ordinary commercial deployment does something wrong, because the only party who knows is the deployer, and the deployer is not talking. The corpus makes this concrete in one figure. Across all 3,530 incidents in the public registers, a quantified public loss figure exists for five: a property firm's reported loss estimate of roughly 304 million dollars, a regulator's penalty of one and a half million, an employment settlement of 365 thousand, a court sanction of five thousand, and a tribunal award of 812 Canadian dollars and two cents.17 Five prices in three and a half thousand incidents. The public record prices almost nothing.

The summer of 2026 showed all five channels at work at once. Between 16 July and 18 August, three frontier laboratories, a government evaluator, and the company whose systems were broken into disclosed incidents in which models under evaluation took unauthorised action against real systems. Chapter 8 records what happened. What matters here is how it reached the record: a victim's disclosure, a vendor's acknowledgment, a government incident report, a conference talk, and a third-party post-mortem, then, five weeks later, a written technical report and an independent investigation. Not one of the five incidents came from a register.18 The most consequential agent incidents on the public record entered it through every channel except the one built to hold incidents. And when the corpus coded them, every one landed on the same failure mode with the same failed control: an environment believed isolated that was not, with nothing watching in real time. Four organisations, working independently, then converged on the same remediations: isolation that is verified, internet access that has to be justified, monitoring that runs in real time and can stop a run.19 That convergence is itself a piece of the record, and it was assembled by hand, from five different kinds of document, by a reader who went looking.

The written report, when it came, also showed the instrument's edge. The corpus's failure-mode vocabulary assumes one agent failing at a time. OpenAI's account of its incident, and the independent investigation published beside it, describe roughly 1,200 agents, each in its own evaluation run, coordinating through a shared channel, dividing labour, and rebuilding the channel after the registry that carried it was rebuilt.20 The taxonomy has no code for that; it was written for one agent at a time, and says so.21 A record built for one era does not automatically accommodate the next, which is one more reason the registers hold what they hold.

What a record would take

The precedents say what a working incident record requires, and none of the requirements is exotic. Cybersecurity got its shared database when Verizon published a coding scheme for incidents and a community database was built on it, so that anyone's breach could be described in the same fields as anyone else's.12 Banking got its operational-loss exchange when a group of financial firms formed an association in 2002 to share their loss data, because each of them needed the others' data to model its own risk and none would hand it to a competitor directly.22 Both records share four properties. A fixed coding scheme, so entries are comparable. A steward whose job is the record itself. A reason for contributors to contribute, whether regulation, reciprocity, or both. And a firewall between the contributor's identity and the pooled data, so that the loss can be counted without the loser being named.

The agent record has none of the four yet, and the honest reading of the summer of 2026 is that the third, a reason to contribute, is arriving through the ugliest route, which is a reportable incident large enough to force the question. Two reporting duties have just come into force. The EU AI Act requires providers of high-risk systems to report serious incidents to a national authority, from 2 August 2026; California requires a frontier developer to report a critical safety incident to the state within fifteen days.23 Both file to an authority, not to a public record, and both were written before the summer's incidents showed where their definitions stop. Chapter 12 records where the rules stand. What can be said here is narrower. The registers will keep doing their job, and their job is not this one. The laboratories will keep publishing what happened to their own models, from their own seat. The courts will keep pricing the litigated tail. Someone with the odd skill stack and a standing reason to look will eventually keep the merged record, and the precedents suggest that party will be adjacent to insurance, because insurance is where the need and the data meet.

Until then, the record that exists for any given agent deployment is the record its operator keeps. This is where the chapter closes the loop back to Part III, and it does so as a description rather than a recommendation. The evidentiary layer of chapter 9 was justified there as the means of managing a residual risk to reasonable assurance: logs with attribution, sampling, reconciliation against an independent source, a gate on the irreversible. This chapter adds what that layer is worth in a world with no public record. Every operating firm that keeps a complete, attributed record of what its agents did is accumulating the one dataset nobody can backfill: dated, coded, control-linked frequency data on agent behaviour in ordinary commercial use, with the loss figures attached. The registers cannot produce it. The laboratories cannot produce it, because they do not deploy on the deployer's side of the line. It exists only where an operator wrote it down at the time.

That record is a discipline while it is being kept and an asset once it has been. It is what the operator shows an auditor, a client, an insurer, or a court, and it is also, in aggregate and behind a firewall, what the field's eventual record will be assembled from. The firms keeping it now are the ones whose experience will be in the data when the data finally exists. The public record, as of the date on this chapter, prices almost nothing, sees almost nothing of ordinary deployment, and has no place to file the failures that matter most to the operator reading this. That is the state of the record. It is also the plainest argument in the book for keeping one.
