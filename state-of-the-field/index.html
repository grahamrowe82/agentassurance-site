Agent Assurance

Installment 12 · 23 September 2026

Where This Is Going

What is left to do

The vocabulary, the controls, and the standards map are enough to run a defensible agent-assurance practice tomorrow. In that narrow sense the work is done.

It is not done in the sense that matters for the field. Everything described so far is a practice, and a practice is what one person or one firm does. A discipline is what a population of practitioners shares. The same words meaning the same things. A way to train the next entrants. A way for buyers to tell a competent practitioner from a confident one without doing the assessment themselves. And a standard the practitioners hold each other to. None of that exists yet for agent assurance. It is being built now, by whoever is doing this work, or it is not being built at all.

Whether any of this hardens into a chartered profession is an open question, and the likelier answer is that it does not. That does not lower the stakes. It only means the work has to be described as it is, which is early, unfinished, and real.

What consolidation requires

Four things separate a practice from a discipline, and each one depends on the one before it. A shared vocabulary, because nothing can be taught, tested, or trusted until practitioners mean the same thing by the same words. A curriculum, because training needs a settled body of knowledge to transmit. Certification, because a certificate is only worth something when it attests to a curriculum a buyer can rely on. And a plain-language layer, because the enterprise version has to be translated for the firms that enterprise grammar was never written for. Here is what exists at each of the four, as of the date on this chapter.

Shared vocabulary

The first requirement is that practitioners can mean the same thing by the same term. Without it there is no training, because there is no settled content to teach. There is no certification, because there is nothing stable to test. And there is no reliable communication between a practitioner and a buyer, because the words on both sides of the table point at different things. Vocabulary is the substrate the rest of the discipline is built on.

NIST published a general-purpose vocabulary for the field, The Language of Trustworthy AI, in March 2023, positioned as a companion to its AI Risk Management Framework.1 It is not a short list. The glossary carries 511 terms with at least one definition and 218 with more than one, drawn from a bibliography of 379 sources.2 A vocabulary that large, published by a national standards body and tied to a framework, is real evidence that the naming work has begun and that it is being done seriously rather than left to whoever writes the loudest blog post.

It is also evidence of how far the work still has to go, because a vocabulary existing is not the same as a vocabulary agreed. The bodies working on AI governance do not yet use the same terms for the same ideas. An International Chamber of Commerce policy paper in 2025 recorded the problem plainly. Different bodies "use different terminologies and methodologies to define AI risks, transparency and robustness as well as security and safety," and it is not yet clear whether those inconsistencies will produce materially different implementations across sectors and jurisdictions.3 A Partnership on AI publication in early 2026 reached the same reading, describing the governance terminology and reporting efforts, including the transparency requirements of the EU's AI Act and the OECD's 2025 work, as still fragmented rather than standardized.4 The vocabulary is being written in several places at once, by bodies that do not yet agree, which is the normal early state of a field and not a sign that anything is wrong.

A practitioner needs a handful of specific words the general AI-governance glossaries do not supply: the verification gap, closure controls, the evidentiary layer, the gate, reasonable assurance imported from audit, accountable human ownership. Using those precisely, and the same way every time, is the smallest unit of consolidation a single practitioner can do, and it is available to anyone. A term takes hold by consistent use, not by announcement, and every practitioner who uses the same word for the same control is doing the vocabulary's work.

A curriculum

The second requirement is a way to train new entrants, which means a settled body of knowledge someone can learn from rather than a set of instincts each practitioner has to rebuild alone. The precedent that maps most closely is information security, which made this exact transition inside living memory and left a clear record of the order it happened in.

The organization that administers the CISSP credential was formed in 1989, and the first Common Body of Knowledge underlying the certification was finalized in 1992, two years before the certification itself launched.5 6 That sequence is the point. The field first wrote down what a competent practitioner is supposed to know, as a shared and referenceable thing, and only then built an examination on top of it. A curriculum is not a course. It is the agreed answer to the question of what the work consists of, stable enough that two practitioners trained separately come out knowing the same things. Agent assurance does not have that yet. A curriculum is a communal artifact, and it forms when enough practitioners converge on the same body of knowledge that a newcomer can be pointed at it and told, learn this.

Certification

The third requirement closes the loop chapter 2 opened. The problem there was that agent-performed work is a credence good: the client cannot evaluate its correctness, security, or governance before delivery, after delivery, possibly ever. The only known answer to a credence-good market is to verify the practitioner rather than the work, because a buyer who cannot assess the thing can still assess the person who made it, if there is a credential that means something. Certification is the instrument that makes that possible at scale, and it is the stage at which a discipline becomes legible to the people who buy from it.

The CISSP launched in 1994 with 46 holders worldwide in its first year.7 By its thirtieth anniversary in 2024 there were more than 165,000 holders globally, and along the way, in 2004, it became the first security certification accredited by the standards body that accredits certifications.8 9 Two numbers, thirty years apart, describe the whole arc: a credential starts as a few dozen people who agree on what competence means, and becomes, if the field it serves keeps growing, the thing a buyer asks for by name. Agent assurance has no such credential for the practitioner today. There is no certificate a client can require of the person, no examination a practitioner can point to, no accredited body standing behind either. What does exist, and arrived first, is certification of the system. AIUC-1, published by the Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company and described by it as the first AI agent standard, certifies a vendor's agent across six categories of risk; its first authorised auditor was named in February 2026, the first certified companies followed within weeks, and insurance structures priced on the certification came with them.10 Chapter 4 records the detail. What it means here is that the assurance market has so far answered the credence problem the way product markets do, by certifying the thing sold, and not yet the way the professions did, by certifying the person. Both answers are legitimate and they are not the same. A certified system says the vendor's agent met a standard on the day it was tested. It says nothing about the operator who deploys it, configures it, and stands behind its output, which is the seat this book is written to. That seat is still uncredentialed, and whether it ever gets a credential is the open question the last section returns to.

The folk-vocabulary layer

The fourth requirement is the one most easily forgotten, because it only becomes visible once the first three are working. Enterprise risk grammar, the standards and frameworks and certifications a large organization uses, is written for large organizations. It assumes a compliance function, a security team, a budget for external assessment, and a reader who already speaks the language. Many of the operators this book describes have none of that. The principal of a ten-person firm has no compliance function at all; a department head inside a large organization is outside the reach of the one that exists. A discipline that stops at the enterprise version has consolidated for the firms that least need help and skipped the ones that most do. The last stage of consolidation is translation: taking the same concepts and rendering them for a scale that enterprise grammar was never built to address.

NIST publishes its full Cybersecurity Framework for organizations with the capacity to use it, In February 2024 it also published a Small Business Quick-Start Guide. That is explicitly a supplement, translating the framework for small organizations with modest or no existing security program, not a replacement for the full text.11 A 2025 draft of NIST's small-business cybersecurity guidance goes further into plain language, using colloquial terms for its audience of very small firms, including calling them "solopreneurs" rather than reaching for a regulatory noun.12 There are 34.8 million small businesses in the United States, 99 percent of all US businesses, and 81.7 percent of them are non-employer firms with no paid staff other than the owners.13 A framework that reaches only the enterprise has, by that count, reached a small fraction of the organizations that run on the technology it governs. The folk-vocabulary layer is how a discipline reaches the rest, and for agent assurance it does not exist yet because the layers above it are still forming.

What the early practitioners owe

A discipline in this state, before its vocabulary has settled and its curriculum exists, depends on the conduct of the people practising it early, in a way a mature field does not. When there is an accredited body and a settled canon, an individual practitioner's choices matter less, because the institutions carry the standard. When there are no institutions yet, the standard is whatever the early practitioners actually do. That places three specific obligations on this generation.

The first is to publish the pattern rather than hoard it. The instinct in a young and lucrative field is to treat method as a trade secret, on the theory that what you know is your edge. For agent assurance the theory is wrong, and it is wrong for the same reason the adoption doctrine is right. The durable edge is not the pattern, which anyone can copy once it is written down. It is the accountability and standing that come from having run it, which cannot be copied. Publishing the pattern is how a curriculum forms, and a practitioner who withholds it is not protecting an advantage so much as slowing the consolidation the whole field, including that practitioner, depends on.

The second obligation is to keep client detail private. Publishing the pattern does not mean publishing the client. The disclosure boundary is that the practitioner's own operations are the exhibit and the clients are absent from it, no identities, no incidents, no engagement detail. This is not in tension with the first obligation; it is what makes the first obligation safe to meet. A practitioner can describe a control, a failure class, a method, and a worked example drawn from their own firm without ever exposing the people who trusted them with the work. The pattern is publishable precisely because it can be separated from the client, and keeping that separation clean is part of what distinguishes a discipline from an industry of anecdotes.

The third obligation is to document incidents honestly, and it is the most consequential, because the field's entire evidence base is thin and forming now. The public record of AI incidents is growing: Stanford's 2026 AI Index reports that documented AI incidents rose to 362 in its most recent count, up from 233 the year before.14 A rising count is not only a sign of more failures; it is also a sign of more being written down, which is what an evidence base is made of. But the record of how those failures were governed, the part a discipline actually needs, is much thinner than the record that they happened. In the AAA-ICDR survey discussed below, only 22 percent of respondents were very confident they could produce evidence of their governance decisions if an auditor or regulator asked.15 That is the gap the early practitioners fill, one honest post-mortem at a time. Every incident documented straight, with the missing control named and the failure class identified, becomes a citation someone can build on. Every one that is buried is a fact the discipline does not get to have. State what failed, state why, state the control that would have caught it, and leave the drama out.

Refusal as a professional obligation

There is a fourth thing the early practitioners owe, and it is different in kind from the other three. A discipline has to be able to refuse. Specifically, it has to be able to decline to certify what it cannot stand behind. A field that cannot say no to work, no to a claim, no to a certification it has not earned the right to give, is a vendor, not a profession.

Refusal has appeared twice already, as the runtime gate of chapter 9 and as the engagement's defensible no in chapter 6, and it is the same reflex each time. The professional altitude is that move made by the field about itself. A discipline that certifies whatever it is paid to certify has no standard, and a credential that is never withheld means nothing when it is granted. A field's authority comes from what it declines as much as from what it approves. The practitioners building the standard now set that reflex or fail to, and a standard built without it will not be worth holding.

Where the field stands

The previous chapter surveyed what the world has written down about agent incidents and why the record is as thin as it is. This one surveys the rest of the field: the standards, the governance that exists on paper and in practice, the rules for reporting, and the people. The reading is dated the moment it is written, and it will be more dated than most, because the field is moving quickly and the record is early.

Standards exist, and they are being adopted. NIST released its AI Risk Management Framework in January 2023, through a consensus-driven, open process, and it has not sat still since.16 In December 2025 NIST released a preliminary draft of a Cybersecurity Framework Profile for Artificial Intelligence, opening a 45-day public comment period, and more than 6,500 individuals had joined the community of interest that helped develop it.17 Alongside the NIST work sits the international management standard for AI, ISO/IEC 42001, and the wider standards map chapter 7 set out. The raw material of the discipline, the standard components it tells practitioners to adopt rather than invent, is real and increasingly available. A practitioner arriving now is not waiting for the standards to exist. They exist.

What the standards have not yet produced is effective governance on the ground, and the survey evidence on that gap is consistent enough to take seriously. An AAA-ICDR survey of 500 senior legal and executive leaders at large North American organizations, most with revenue above a billion dollars, found that 87 percent reported some form of AI governance in place, while only 22 percent said those systems were operating effectively.18 The same survey found only 33 percent had defined escalation pathways for when AI systems misbehave.19 ISACA's 2026 poll of more than 3,400 digital-trust professionals found only 38 percent of organizations had a formal, comprehensive AI policy, up from 28 percent a year earlier, with 25 percent having no active policy at all.20 The same poll found that 39 percent of respondents did not know whether they had a documented process for shutting down or overriding an AI system if something went wrong. More than half did not know how long it would take to halt one during a security incident.21 Governance exists on paper far more often than it works in practice, and the basic operational controls, the ability to escalate, to produce evidence, to shut a system down, are missing in a large fraction of even the well-resourced organizations that have governance at all.

An academic reading of the same period puts a name to what that gap is made of. A 2026 analysis of AI Risk Management Framework adoption concluded that the primary obstacle to effective AI governance is not a lack of standards but inconsistent and incomplete organizational implementation of the standards that already exist.22 That finding is the field's condition stated precisely, and it is the condition this discipline exists to work in. The problem is not that nobody has written down what good governance looks like. The problem is that writing it down has not made it happen, and the distance between a standard on a shelf and a control that runs is exactly the distance an agent-assurance practitioner is paid to close.

There is a practitioner population forming to close it. An IAPP survey conducted with Credo AI drew more than 670 respondents across 45 countries and territories, of whom 77 percent said their organization was working on AI governance.23 The same survey found that 23.5 percent cited difficulty finding qualified AI-governance professionals as part of their staffing challenge.24 A field where most organizations are working on the problem and a quarter cannot find people qualified to do it is a field with more demand than supply, which is the ordinary condition of a discipline in formation. It is also, read plainly, an invitation: the work exists, the organizations that need it know they need it, and there are not enough people who can do it.

What does not exist yet is as important to state as what does. There is no certification specific to agent assurance, no accredited examination, no settled curriculum, and no single vocabulary all the standards bodies share. There is no agreed body of terms for the newer problems either. As agent deployments scale from one agent to many, the governance of collective autonomy, the controls for many agents whose individually reasonable actions can sum to an unreasonable result, is a control family the field has barely named. The vocabulary for it is not settled, because the vocabulary for the simpler single-agent case is not settled. These are the edges of the field, the places where the work is most obviously unfinished. A new practitioner should know they are arriving before the credential, before the canon, and before some of the vocabulary, because that is both the honest state of things and the reason there is so much to do. The rules for telling anyone are unfinished too, and the summer of 2026 showed where. When OpenAI's models broke out of an evaluation and into Hugging Face's systems (chapter 8 records the incident), the three state laws that require a frontier developer to report a critical safety incident, California's SB 53, New York's RAISE Act, and Illinois SB 315, defined the reportable categories identically. Three of the four require death, serious injury to more than fifty people, or more than a billion dollars of damage. The fourth, control subversion, applies only outside an evaluation designed to elicit the behaviour. A legal analysis published three days after the disclosure concluded that OpenAI might not have been legally required to disclose the incident at all.25 It disclosed anyway, on 21 July, and on 22 August asked California to amend the law to require monitoring of frontier models under training or evaluation for potential serious incidents.26 Twenty-eight members of Congress wrote on 10 August asking for the logs and for answers to twenty-three questions.27 A federal bill introduced in July, the FRONTIER Act, was being lined up for containment language at a September markup, and Hugging Face's policy lead asked publicly for standardized disclosure requirements.28 The pattern is the one chapter 4 documents: the incident arrives first and the rule for reporting it is written afterwards. A practitioner working now works in that interval, which is why the third obligation above, the honest post-mortem, is stated as an obligation and not left to the law.

What to do first

For someone arriving now, here is a short list of first actions, each available today and none waiting on the consolidation the rest of this chapter described.

Adopt the vocabulary. Use the terms in their canonical forms, in client memos and proposals and in the way the practice describes its own service, as if the words were already standard. This is the smallest act of consolidation and the one entirely within a single practitioner's control. It costs nothing, it makes the practitioner legible to everyone else using the same words, and it is how the vocabulary takes hold, by usage rather than announcement.

Apply the standards map. The seventh chapter's adoption doctrine is not a thing to build toward; it is a thing to start with. The standard components exist, they are increasingly adopted, and the discipline is assembly before it is invention. A practitioner beginning now should reach for the information-security standards, the AI-management frameworks, the continuity and sanitization standards, and the open contract libraries first, and treat anything self-built as the standing weakness it is. The advantage of arriving after the standards were written is that there is less to invent than the founders had to.

Start the evidence discipline on day one. This is the one that is hardest to retrofit and cheapest to begin. A practice maintained as policy-plus-log from the start finds that when certification or an audit or a due-diligence questionnaire arrives, the answer is a purchase decision rather than a project. A practitioner who keeps the logs, records the sign-offs, drills the restore, and documents the controls from the first client has evidence when it is asked for. A practitioner who means to start later discovers, at the audit cliff chapter 1 named, that the evidence for the period before they started does not exist and cannot be reconstructed. The evidentiary layer is not a thing to add when the practice is mature. It is the thing that makes the practice mature, and it is available from the first day of the first engagement.

The capabilities that AI has made cheap and abundant, writing code quickly, producing volumes of text, generating marketing output, are exactly the capabilities whose value is heading toward zero, because the technology that made the practitioner good at them makes everyone good at them. Building a practice on those is building on the part of the work that is being commoditized fastest. Chapter 5 set out the economics: when intelligence collapses in price, value migrates to what stays scarce, which is accountability, verified context, and standing. The durable practice is built on the complements to the cheap capability, not on the capability itself. Assurance is a complement. It is worth more precisely as the thing it assures becomes cheaper, because the cheaper and more abundant agent-produced work becomes, the more it needs someone accountable standing behind it. That is the ground to build on.

The standards bodies take public comment and build communities of interest; the NIST profile drew more than six and a half thousand participants, and that participation is open. The professional associations forming around AI governance run surveys, publish research, and convene the people doing the work. None of these is yet the accredited home the field will eventually have, because that home does not exist. But they are where the vocabulary is being argued, the standards drafted, and the practitioners found, and a person who wants to help build the discipline rather than only practise it can find the work being done in the open and join it.

The claim the discipline rests on

Agent assurance may never become a profession in the full sense, with a licence and a chartering body and a monopoly on the work. The more likely outcome is that it does not, because the field moves faster than universities and licensing bodies form, and it will more plausibly resemble cybersecurity: a large and lasting market of competing standards, certifications, and practices rather than a single chartered institution. Cybersecurity never chartered, and the demand for it only grew.

That outcome does not diminish the work. There is a great deal to do, it is difficult, and it matters. More and more of the work the world relies on is being done by agents whose output no client can verify alone. Someone accountable has to do it well, under conditions that will not hold still. The vocabulary, the controls, the history, the economics, the state of the record: these are the parts that could be written down now. The rest, the curriculum, the certification, the settled terms, the folk-vocabulary layer that reaches the smallest firms, is the work still in front of the practitioners. It is being built now, by them, or it is not being built at all.
