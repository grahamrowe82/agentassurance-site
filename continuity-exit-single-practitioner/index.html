Agent Assurance

Installment 10 · 9 September 2026

The Day You Are Not There

One person away

A single practitioner or a two-person shop is, on any given morning, one person away from total discontinuity. The controls run because someone maintains them. The logs are complete because someone designed the table and checks that it fills. The gate refuses the irreversible action because someone decided which actions are irreversible and wired the checkpoint. Remove that person, by illness, by accident, by a decision to leave the field, and every control described in this book so far becomes a set of files that no one can operate and no one can read. The client is left holding an agent-run system it never understood, with the one person who understood it gone.

The controls in this chapter answer that condition. They are key-person continuity, client exit, vendor concentration, and a reserve held against all three. None of them improves the day-to-day work. They keep the day-to-day work, and the evidence it produced, alive across a change of hands: a handover, a wind-down, a client leaving, a vendor withdrawing, the practitioner stepping away for good. They are the least visible controls in the discipline and the most often missing, and their presence or absence is the plainest line between a fiduciary-grade practice and a competent freelancer who happens to do similar work. The freelancer's clients depend on the freelancer. The practice's clients depend on the practice, which is a different thing precisely to the degree that these controls exist.

The professions that got here first drew the same line, and they drew it in their rulebooks rather than their aspirations. A small AI-agent practice is not the first kind of firm to be one person away from stranding its clients, and it does not have to invent the answer. This chapter covers the practitioner failing. The other way a small practice's assurance decays, a signature that ages while the practitioner is still present, is the custodial engagement of chapter 6, and the two are meant to be read together.

The sharpest public record of the one-person-away failure is not in professional-services firms at all; it is in software, where much of the world runs on libraries maintained by one or two unpaid people. In 2016 a single developer, in a naming dispute, unpublished an eleven-line package from a public registry. Thousands of projects depended on it, including the Babel compiler and the React framework. None could be built or installed. The registry afterwards changed its rules so that a depended-on package could not simply vanish.1 When the Heartbleed flaw was found in the cryptographic library securing millions of websites, it emerged that the project had been running on about two thousand dollars a year in donations. An industry consortium had to be formed to fund two full-time developers after the fact.2 And the 2024 backdoor in the XZ compression library was made possible less by a clever exploit than by a continuity gap. The library had a single original maintainer. A patient multi-year campaign run through fake accounts pressured him into handing maintainer rights to the party that then inserted the backdoor.3 The attacker there was an external hazard; the opening was the absence of the controls this chapter is about, a critical system resting on one tired volunteer with no vetted succession and no second pair of hands.

That record belongs here because an agent-operated practice is a software maintainer now too. It maintains the generated systems it runs for its clients, and it inherits the maintainer's continuity problem without even open source's thin safety nets. No public registry to restore what was lost, no consortium to fund the gap, no crowd of downstream users to notice that the project went dark. The controls that follow are how a practice keeps from becoming the next one-person-away story.

Key-person continuity: the analysis first

The first control is not a backup or a document. It is an analysis, and it comes before either, because you cannot arrange continuity for a stoppage you have not named.

Business continuity is an established field with its own standard, which defines the aim as an organization's capability to keep delivering its products and services within acceptable timeframes, at a predefined capacity, during a disruption.4 The method that field uses to get there is a business-impact analysis. It is a systematic pass over everything the practice does. Of each function it asks what stops if the practitioner is unavailable, who notices, how quickly it hurts, and how long each stoppage can be tolerated before it becomes a breach of an obligation the client is counting on. For an agent-assurance practice the analysis is specific. If the practitioner is gone for a week, which client agents keep running unattended, and which of them take actions that need a human decision the gate is holding open? If a model provider changes something on a Tuesday and the practitioner is in a hospital, whose reconciliation silently stops matching, and how long before anyone sees it? The output of the analysis is a short, ordered list: the functions that fail first, the ones that can wait, and the time each has before its failure reaches a client. Everything else in this section is built to that list.

A solo bankruptcy attorney in the Kansas City area, more than twenty years in practice, was diagnosed with a recurrence of cancer. From her hospital bed she transferred her cases to another attorney under a signed agreement, which is more than most solo practitioners arrange, and it was still not enough. The successor took on sixteen active bankruptcy cases, several in states of emergency and most with no viable way to recover a fee.5 She was paid, in part, on two of those sixteen; the rest she worked largely without pay, with help from volunteers.6 Nothing here is fraud or neglect. It is a capable professional who did the responsible thing at the last possible moment, and discovered that a last-moment handover of live, time-critical work is a burden the receiver largely absorbs and the clients partly wear. The analysis done in advance is what turns that scramble into a procedure.

The professions that see this pattern often enough eventually write it into the rules. The bar associations did. An American Bar Association ethics opinion holds that lawyers must plan in advance to protect client files and designate another attorney with authority to review the files and notify clients in the event of the lawyer's death or disability.7 California's rules of professional conduct go further and read the ordinary duty of diligence as potentially requiring every sole practitioner to prepare a written plan. It would name another competent lawyer to review client files, notify each client of the practitioner's death, and determine whether any matter needs immediate protective action.8 A professional body decided that continuity is not a personal courtesy the practitioner may extend to clients but a duty the practitioner owes them, enforceable, written down in advance, with a named successor. An agent-assurance practice holds work that is at least as opaque to the client as a live bankruptcy case and a good deal harder for an arbitrary successor to pick up cold. The duty transfers directly.

Key-person loss is rarely a clean single event. A study of major law-firm collapses found that failure runs as a self-reinforcing spiral its authors call a partner run. It begins with the departure of one senior figure. In the six months before a firm ceases operations it loses on average about eleven percent of its partners, and almost sixteen percent in the year before.9 The mechanism is that each departure makes the next one more rational, until the firm empties faster than it can respond. A one-person practice has no partners to run, but the physics is the same. The practitioner's absence is the first of a cascade. Clients who lose confidence leave, the revenue that funded the handover goes with them, and the successor inherits less runway exactly when the work is heaviest. Continuity planning that assumes a single, orderly failure is planning for the easy case. The analysis should assume the hard one.

Break-glass: access granted once, and reviewed after

Once the analysis names the functions that must survive the practitioner's absence, the next question is mechanical: how does anyone else get in? An agent-assurance practice runs on credentials, to model providers, to client systems, to the deployment substrate, to the logs. In normal operation those credentials are the practitioner's, scoped tightly and used daily. In an emergency the nominated successor needs them, and the design problem is to make that access available when it is needed without making it casually available the rest of the time.

The security field has a name for this and a worked pattern: break-glass access. The pattern is not a spare key under a mat. A hospital faces an identical tension: a clinician in a genuine emergency must reach a record now, and the same reach must never become the routine way in. Yale's published procedure for emergency access to protected health records designates a specific role, an emergency-account manager, responsible for distributing the emergency accounts. It separates two duties on principle. Each use of an emergency account is reviewed afterward, and the person who created the account is not the person who reviews the audit trail of its use.10 The account does not stay live. The guidance is to deactivate it on a short fixed window, on the order of hours to a day. The access is then reconciled back to the name of the person who actually used it, so the record shows a human, not a generic emergency identity.11

Transposed to a small practice, the control is a defined, auditable mechanism by which a nominated party, a spouse, a trusted colleague, a professional executor, can obtain the access continuity requires. The conditions are written in advance: what triggers it, who authorizes it, what they are permitted to reach, how quickly the grant expires, and who reviews the trail afterward. Break-glass is not weaker access hastily arranged; it is stronger access, because every use of it is expected, bounded, and examined. A credential that a successor can use once, in a defined emergency, with the use reviewed after, is a control. A credential that a successor happens to know because it was emailed to them years ago is the absence of one.

Escrow: the deposit and the verification that it is real

Break-glass gets a person in. It does not, on its own, guarantee there is anything intact for them to find. The client's critical code, its configuration, and the keys to operate it have to exist somewhere the practitioner's own disappearance cannot erase, held by a party whose continuity is independent of the practice. That is escrow, and here too the mechanism is old and specified.

Software escrow, where a neutral third party holds a vendor's source code and releases it to the customer under defined conditions, has been standard practice in software procurement for decades. The mature form of it has learned not to trust the deposit. Virginia's state IT-procurement agency, in its escrow guidance, requires the agreement to release the deposited materials to the customer on named trigger events. They are the supplier's failure to meet its support obligations for sixty days, the supplier's breach or default, the supplier's bankruptcy, or the supplier ceasing to do business in the ordinary course.12 Those triggers are the continuity conditions written as contract terms, and an agent-assurance practice's escrow arrangement needs its own version of them, because "the practitioner is unavailable" has to be defined precisely enough that a third party can act on it without a judgment call.

The same guidance does not accept that code placed in escrow is usable code. It requires the escrow agent to verify every deposit within thirty days, testing it for completeness, accuracy, and functionality, and to deliver a written report of the tests within ten business days of finishing.13 The failure mode of escrow is not the vault; it is the day the vault is opened and the deposit turns out to be a stale, incomplete, or unbuildable copy of a system that has moved on without it. A practice that escrows a client's deployment and keys inherits that failure mode exactly. The deposit is worth what it can be restored to, and the only way to know what it can be restored to is to have restored it, on a schedule, and kept the report. An escrow that has never been opened and tested is a promise, not a control. The verification is what makes it one.

Continuity instructions, with the same formality as a will

The analysis, the break-glass mechanism, and the escrow are operational arrangements. They need a legal and organizational instruction that sits above them and says what is to happen to the practice itself: continued, sold, or wound down, by whom, under what authority, with what obligations to which clients. The professions that treat continuity as a duty treat this instruction with the formality of estate planning, because that is what it is.

This is the same ground the bar rules were standing on. A written succession plan, a designated successor with actual authority, advance notice to clients: those are not operational conveniences, they are instruments executed with legal effect, prepared before they are needed and kept current.8 The reason to prepare the continuity instruction alongside the practitioner's own will, with the same seriousness, is that the two documents fail the same way when they are left informal. An understanding that a colleague "will sort things out" has no more force than an unwritten wish about an estate, and it lands on the same people at the same bad moment. The instruction should name who takes authority over the practice and its client engagements, and what they are empowered to do. It should say how the escrow and break-glass arrangements are triggered and by whom, and whether the intended path is handover to a named successor or an orderly wind-down. It should be a document a lawyer helped write and that the nominated successor has read and agreed to, not a paragraph in a folder the successor discovers for the first time on the day they need it.

Per-client runbooks

Access, a deposit, and legal authority still leave the hardest gap: knowledge. The successor can get in, can restore the system, and has the standing to act, and none of that tells them what the system does or how this particular client's engagement runs. That knowledge, for most small practices, lives in one head, and the head is the single point of failure the whole chapter is about.

The control is a per-client runbook: documented, current, step-by-step operational knowledge for each engagement, written so that someone other than the practitioner could execute it. What each agent in the client's system is for and what it is scoped to touch. Where the logs are and how to read them. Which actions the gate holds for human sign-off, and who on the client's side is the accountable human. What the reconciliation checks against, and what to do when it stops matching. What the client was told, and what the practice is on the hook for. The test of a runbook is not that it exists but that it is executable by a stranger. Yale's break-glass procedure makes the same point when it allows even a manual, paper log as an acceptable record, provided it captures what a reviewer later needs.10 Completeness beats elegance. A runbook that assumes the reader already knows how the system works is a diary, not a handover.

The runbook is also the artifact that makes every earlier control in this chapter cashable. Break-glass access to a system the successor cannot operate buys nothing. An escrow deposit no one knows how to deploy buys nothing. The continuity instruction that names a successor who cannot actually run the work names a figurehead. The runbook is where the practice's operating knowledge stops being tacit and becomes transferable, which is the property continuity actually requires. It is tedious to keep current, and keeping it current is the work; a runbook that describes last quarter's system is its own kind of hazard, because it will be trusted in an emergency and will be wrong. Keep it in one place and generate every copy from that place, because a set of handover documents maintained by hand drifts apart in ways that reading any one of them will not show.

Client exit as an assurance obligation

Continuity is about the practitioner leaving. Exit is about the client leaving, and it is an assurance obligation in its own right, not a courtesy performed on the way out. Every engagement ends, by completion, by the client moving on, or by the practice winding down, and the terms of the ending are a control surface exactly as much as the terms of the running system. An engagement that cannot be exited cleanly was not fully under control while it ran.

The obligations divide into a small set, and each has a precedent. The first is that the client's data and systems are returned or handed over on a documented path. That is an offboarding runbook, the mirror of the per-client runbook: what the client gets back, in what form, who receives it, and what the practice retains and for how long. The second is deletion, and deletion is where the good intentions of a departing practice most often fail, because deleting data is easy to promise and easy to leave half-done. In a case the Federal Trade Commission settled in 2025, a hacker entered the database of Illuminate Education, an education-technology provider, using the credentials of a former employee, credentials that were still live long after the employee had gone. The records of around ten million students were exposed. The same provider had left flagged vulnerabilities unremediated and student data in plain text.14 Access that is not decommissioned is a door left open, and the person who walks through it later will not be the person you offboarded. In another, the same commission found that InMarket Media's policy of retaining location data for five years was unnecessary for the purpose the data had been collected for, and ordered it to delete the data it had kept and anything derived from it unless it obtained the consumers' consent.15 Retention past the point of purpose is not neutral; it is a standing liability, and an exit is the natural moment to discharge it. The Solicitors Regulation Authority's accounts rules make the same demand for client money, requiring that funds be returned promptly to the client as soon as there is no longer any proper reason to hold them.16 Data held past the engagement is the same obligation in a different asset.

Deletion, done as a control, produces evidence. The established form is a certificate of destruction, and there is a standard template for it. NIST's media-sanitization guideline includes a sample certificate of sanitization, offered explicitly as an example of the information such an attestation should collect rather than as a mandated form.17 It records the method, classified into one of four defined categories, the specific tool and version used, and how the result was verified. It separates two signatures: the person who performed the sanitization, who signs a personal attestation that the record is accurate, and a second validator, each with their own name and organization.18 That structure is the exit control in miniature: a deletion is not something the practice asserts happened, it is something a named person attests to and a second person validates, on a form that records enough to be disputed. The practice that deletes a departing client's data and keeps a completed certificate has evidence it can show. The practice that deletes the data and sends an email saying so has a claim.

The last exit obligation is the one that survives the handshake: backups. Data returned and primary copies deleted still leaves the backups, which by design outlive the systems they protect, and an exit that does not specify when the backups expire has not actually ended. The offboarding runbook should state the retention on every backup that holds the client's data and the date each one is gone, so that the deletion attested on the certificate is not silently undone by a restore six months later. Documented backup expiry is the difference between data that is deleted and data that is merely out of sight.

Vendor concentration and the cost of leaving

The exit obligations so far run from the client to the practice. There is a mirror-image dependency running from the practice to its vendors, and for an agent-assurance practice it is acute, because the whole practice sits on a small number of model providers and platforms whose decisions it does not control. Vendor concentration is the risk that the practice and its clients are locked to one supplier so completely that leaving is not a real option, and it should be managed as a control before a vendor's decision forces the question.

The UK Competition and Markets Authority, examining the cloud-infrastructure market, found Amazon Web Services and Microsoft dominating, each holding up to forty percent of national customer spend. It provisionally concluded that technical and commercial barriers, including fees charged simply to move data out, make it difficult for customers to switch and lock them into their initial choices.19 The difficulty is not only structural but underestimated by the people exposed to it. In a survey of 542 enterprise AI executives, eighty-nine percent believed they could switch AI vendors within a month. Of those who had actually tried to migrate, fifty-eight percent said the process either failed or took far more effort than expected.20 The gap between the eighty-nine percent who think they can leave and the majority of movers who found they could not is the whole risk in one line. The cost of avoiding a switch is quantified where a public buyer has had to weigh it. The US Government Accountability Office found that the Department of Agriculture had paid over a hundred million dollars more to stay with an incumbent office suite specifically to avoid the even higher cost of switching away from it.21 Lock-in is not a future tax. It is a present price, paid to postpone a move that gets more expensive the longer it is postponed.

Not every field is equally trapped. At the easy end is work that standard formats have made portable almost by default. Business accounting is close to this: the US Internal Revenue Service states plainly that it can accept and read the backup files of the accounting software most businesses use,22 which is possible only because the underlying records are held in forms that many tools can read. A client can move between the common accounting platforms with real friction but no catastrophe, because the data speaks a shared language.23 In the middle sits professional work whose relationship is sticky but whose records are portable by right. A client leaving a law firm takes the file. The same professional-conduct machinery that obliges a departed practitioner's successor to hand client files back, the succession duties earlier in this chapter, makes the documents the client's own to move, even when the working relationship was closer to a marriage than a subscription. The relationship does not transfer; the records do. At the hard end is the case where a firm holds another firm's crown jewels in a form only that firm can operate. That is the IT-outsourcing trap in its classic shape, and it is the shape agent-assurance work most resembles when it is done carelessly. When the City of Munich set out to escape Microsoft's product decisions after support for its operating system ended, its own officials described the lesson in one sentence: Microsoft had shown them what it meant to be dependent on a vendor.24 Leaving was possible. It was not cheap, and the reason it was affordable at all was that they had decided to control their own path rather than remain subject to someone else's lifecycle.

The control that keeps a practice near the easy end of that gradient is portability by construction. The assets an agent-assurance practice builds, the prompts, the workflows, the evaluations that test them, should be held as versioned artifacts in forms the practice owns and can move, not as configuration trapped inside one vendor's console. A prompt kept in a file under version control moves; a prompt that exists only inside a provider's proprietary interface does not. An evaluation suite the practice can run against a different model is a bridge to that model; one that can only run inside the incumbent's tooling is a wall around it. The discipline is to treat every vendor as replaceable in principle and to keep the artifacts that make replacement possible current, so that switching is a project the practice could choose to run rather than a cliff it cannot approach. This is the substrate doctrine carried into the time dimension: components chosen for a legible, portable surface are also the components a practice can leave.

The reserve

The last control is money, set aside for a defined purpose and held apart from the practitioner's own finances. Every continuity and exit obligation in this chapter costs something to discharge. A handover takes weeks of a successor's paid time. A wind-down has to fund the deletion, the certificates, the final client communications, and the run-off of obligations that outlast the last invoice. Break-glass, escrow, and runbooks are cheap to hold and expensive to exercise, and they are exercised exactly when revenue has stopped. A reserve is the control that makes the other controls affordable at the moment they are needed, and without it the continuity plan is a set of instructions no one can pay to follow.

The professions that require continuity also require the money to fund it, and they specify it. The Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors requires that a firm ceasing to trade maintain fully retroactive professional-indemnity cover for consumer claims of a defined minimum for at least six years from the expiry of the policy in force at cessation.25 The obligation to be covered does not end when the firm ends. It runs for years past the last day of trading, and the firm is required, in advance, to have arranged and funded that tail. The UK Financial Conduct Authority's client-money rules make the same move in a different register, requiring firms to reconcile client money against what they owe at least every twenty-five business days and to hold a defined minimum of capital when they hold client money in certain forms.26 The common principle across both is that an obligation which survives the practice has to be funded before the practice needs the funds, because the moment of need is precisely the moment of no income.

For a small AI-agent practice the reserve should be sized and held for the same purpose: to fund the continuity or wind-down obligations this chapter describes, at the point where they fall due and the practice is not earning. The right size is not a fixed ratio, because the obligations that have to be funded depend on the shape of the practice, the number of live client engagements, the length of the tail on each, and the practitioner's own circumstances. A reserve is adequate when it can carry the practice's specific continuity plan to completion. That number comes from the business-impact analysis at the start of this chapter. Sizing the reserve is the analysis closing its own loop. The reserve is not savings the practitioner might draw on in a good year, it is capital committed to obligations that will outlive the practice, held apart and not spent, for the same reason a departing surveyor's run-off cover is not optional.

The line the discipline draws

The controls in this chapter do not make the work better on any ordinary day. They make the practice survivable: survivable of the practitioner's absence, of a client's departure, of a vendor's withdrawal, and of the practice's own eventual end. That is their whole function, and it is why they are the controls most often missing from a small practice and the ones that most sharply distinguish a fiduciary-grade practice from a competent freelancer doing similar work. The freelancer is a single point of failure whose clients discover the fact only when it fails. The practice has done the analysis, arranged the access, tested the deposit, written the runbook, specified the exit, kept the assets portable, and funded the reserve. The same failure is then a procedure with a known cost and a named successor, rather than a client left stranded with a system no one can run.

The evidentiary layer exists so that what an agent did can be reconstructed, examined, and defended after the fact, and the gate exists so that the irreversible action does not happen without a human standing behind it. Both are only as durable as the practice that maintains them. A logging table no one can read, a gate no successor can operate, a record whose meaning left with the practitioner: these are the evidentiary layer failing not at the moment of an incident but at the moment of a handover. Continuity is the evidentiary layer's own continuity. The record has to survive the recorder, or it was never the durable evidence the discipline claimed.

None of these controls promises that a handover will be painless or that a client will never be inconvenienced by a practitioner's absence. What they promise is that the risk of discontinuity was managed rather than ignored. A specific, funded, tested plan exists. The obligations to the client were carried past the end of the engagement and past the end of the practice. Someone accountable arranged all of it in advance. That is reasonable assurance applied to the life of the practice itself,27 and it is the difference between a practice a client can rely on and one that merely works while the practitioner is present. The discipline draws its line here, at the arrangements that outlast the person, and a practice that has not drawn it has left its most important control unbuilt.
