Chapter 3: The Answers Already Exist

The finding

Trust used to live in certificates, and it still can. This is how I found out what they are for.

I ran a healthcare company for years, a nursing call centre advising people with chronic disease on managing their condition, and we were audited constantly. You learn what a standard operating procedure is, you write them, and it is baffling why they exist. In one of my first audits the auditor asked to see my standard operating procedure for standard operating procedures. I laughed, because it was the most Dilbert thing I had ever heard. The auditor did not laugh. I got a finding, and I spent the weekend writing a document about how we would write other documents.

At the time it seemed absurd. What we were trying to do was help sick people. In retrospect it was fundamental, and the reason is what the auditor was actually asking. A call centre has to know that every nurse tells every patient the right thing, and has to still know it next year, after the nurse who wrote the script has moved on. That document was how the business knew what it knew. Almost nobody writes one until they get burnt, or until a customer who has been burnt makes them.

Who gets there

Most businesses never get big or complicated enough to need it. We got there because we had large, regulated customers who had been burnt before and passed the lesson down to us in the form of an audit. Anyone pressing enter in an AI development environment gets there in an afternoon. They build the complexity, the danger, and the responsibility that should force the document, and they have no customer to force it, so it never gets written. The weight accumulates all the same, with nobody to point at it.

From the outside all of this looks hostile, bureaucratic, and pointless, and I thought so too. The open-ended, generous creativity of AI grows into an octopus of obligations and confusion, and what looked like the terrifying bureaucracy of passing an audit turns out to be the medicine. It is the best thing you can do for yourself. It protects you, it protects the business, and it is the only way to actually understand what you have built, instead of outsourcing your understanding to something that does not exist and is only statistics running in a data centre somewhere.

What I am actually saying

Writing a procedure for procedures is a small part of it. Learn what an ISO standard is, and what SOC 2 is. Embrace standards, audits, and all the other things you imagine are the province of big listed companies, as fervently as you embrace pressing enter in your coding tool. Whatever you create is going to need governing, and human beings have already worked out how to govern things. They did it the hard way, one failure at a time, and wrote down what they learned, and the writing is sitting there with a name on it.

The names matter, because they are the names of the solutions to the problems you have now and the problems you are going to have. ISO 27001 is how an organisation manages the security of its information, written down and checked by someone from outside. SOC 2 is the report an auditor writes about how a company looks after other people's data, and it is what your bigger customers will eventually ask you for.

GAMP is how the pharmaceutical industry proves its computer systems do what they claim. ISO 9001 is how a business shows it does things the same way every time.

There are others, and it is worth giving the full list, because each one is the answer to a problem you now have. Customer data in a database you built: ISO 27001, and Cyber Essentials for the basics if you are in the UK. A bigger customer asking how you look after their data: SOC 2. Personal data moving through your systems: the GDPR and its impact assessment, and ISO 27701 for the management of it. A process that has to be right every time: ISO 9001. Software in a regulated industry, medicines or devices: GAMP.

Numbers that reach your accounts from software you wrote: the controls over financial reporting that accountants have run since Sarbanes-Oxley in 2002, including the ones for spreadsheets. Something the business cannot run without: ISO 22301, business continuity. The AI itself: ISO 42001, published at the end of 2023, and the EU AI Act, whose obligations arrive between 2025 and 2027. A regulated customer will ask about the NIST cybersecurity framework. Every one of these is a list of questions, and the questions are the point.

Where the questions came from

The thing to understand about those questions is where they came from. Every standard is a gift handed down by people that horrible things happened to, who then sat down and wrote out what should have been done so that nobody after them had to suffer the same way. The forms look boring, and I thought so too. Each line on them was the worst day of somebody's professional life, and the writing down was their attempt to help you. It is hard-won knowledge disguised as a form to fill in.

Take three lines from ISO 27001. One asks whether you remove people's access when they leave. In 2018 an engineer who had resigned from Cisco months earlier logged into the company's cloud account, which still accepted his credentials, and deleted 456 virtual machines running its Webex Teams service. Sixteen thousand customer accounts were down for up to two weeks, and the case is on the record because the US Department of Justice prosecuted him in 2020.

Another asks whether you keep a backup off the network. In June 2017 the NotPetya malware wiped every Windows machine at Maersk, the world's largest container shipping company, in a few hours, and with them every copy of the directory that held the company's identities. One copy survived, on a domain controller in Ghana that had been offline through a power cut. Wired's account of the recovery in 2018 records the company flying that hard drive to London, and a cost of around three hundred million dollars.

A third asks whether you control changes to systems that are running. On 1 August 2012 Knight Capital deployed new trading software to seven of its eight servers. The eighth kept running code that had been retired years earlier, and in forty-five minutes it sent millions of orders that lost the firm more than four hundred million dollars. The firm was sold within the year, and the SEC's order of 2013 sets out what happened. Do you have a change management process. That question reads differently once you know who asked it first.

The one thing

None of these was written for AI, and none of them needs to have been. They were written for the situation you are in: a business that depends on systems and information that nobody fully understands, that has to answer to customers, regulators, and its own people for what those systems do. That situation is centuries old. The tool is new, and the situation is the one people have been governing all along.

So this is the one thing I would like to give people, and it is uncomfortable. You are going to suffer. The afternoon of pressing enter turns into months of questionnaires and procedures and documents about documents, and there is no version of this that skips it. But the answers to your problems already exist. They have names, they are written down, and the people who wrote them were trying to help you.
